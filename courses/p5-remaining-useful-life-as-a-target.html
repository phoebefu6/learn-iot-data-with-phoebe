Remaining useful life as a target

Session 1 found the label hiding in the cycle count: life minus cycle. Tonight asks whether to use it as it is. Written raw, it asks a model to tell an engine with 361 cycles left from one with 127 while both still look healthy. Capped, it asks only for a countdown near the end. Where to cap is a convention, 125 in some papers and 130 in others, and the bench runs both. Underneath sits the floor: the fleet average minus age, the score of knowing nothing. Real public rows from NASA's simulation, scored in your browser.

Part 0

Harrow Lane's planners ask for one number

Harrow Lane Overhaul is a constructed engine-overhaul shop, and its fleet is NASA's 100 simulated engines. Every week its planners decide which engines to pull. They ask the analyst for one number per engine: how many cycles are left. Before any model, she has to decide what that number means early in an engine's life, when nothing has started to wear, and what the dumbest honest answer scores. Both decisions are tonight.

Live = presented in the 45 minutes Self-study = the full depth, after class 🔍 Every diagram zooms

★ What you walk out with tonight. The uncapped and the capped remaining-useful-life target, drawn against cycles for the shortest and the longest engine in FD001. Why 125 sits just under the shortest training life of 128, and why 130 is in the literature too: a convention, not a law. The floor, the fleet average minus age, at RMSE 45.61 and a summed score of 305,167 that is mostly two long-lived engines. And the first rungs of the ladder rebuilt in your console: 30.50 with the raw target, 24.00 with the capped one, on the same channels and the same least squares, 300 units under test.

LiveWhat the number is for3 min▶

A planner does not need to know that an engine has 300 cycles left rather than 250. Both mean "not this week, not next month". The number starts to matter when the end is close enough to plan around, and there it matters a great deal: too late and the engine fails in service, too early and a healthy one comes off the wing. The target a model is trained on should carry the information the decision needs, no more.

The engines are simulated with C-MAPSS, and the degradation in them is simulated. Every number tonight is computed from real public rows from NASA's simulation, never from real engines, and scored on 30 held-out engines cut at 10 points each, which stand in for the NASA test set and are not comparable with it.

Self-studyThe words tonight3 min▶
  • Uncapped label. Life minus cycle, every row, from the first cycle to the last.
  • Piecewise-linear (capped) label. The same countdown, held flat at the cap early in life.
  • Cap. The flat value. 125 and 130 are both in use on FD001.
  • Floor. The fleet-average model: average training life minus cycles seen, floored at zero. No sensors.
  • Rung. One model on the bench's ladder. Rung 0 is the floor, rung 1 the raw linear fit, rung 2 the capped one.
  • Cut point. A cycle at which a held-out engine is stopped and the model asked for its remaining life. 300 of them, seed 2008.
  • Bias. Mean of predicted minus true. Positive means late, the side the asymmetric score punishes harder.
Part 1 · covers the target

Uncapped or capped: what you ask the model to learn

12 min live

Two engines bracket FD001: engine 39, the shortest, fails at cycle 128; engine 69, the longest, at 362. The figure draws both labels against cycles, then the capped version of engine 69's. The triangle between the black line and the copper one is everything the cap throws away.

LiveWhat the uncapped label asks for4 min▶

At cycle 1, engine 69's label is 361 and engine 39's is 127. The readme says each engine "starts with different degrees of initial wear and manufacturing variation which is unknown to the user", and that it operates normally at the start before a fault develops. So a model fed cycle 1 of each sees two healthy engines and is asked to put them 234 cycles apart. Engine 39 does start a little hotter, which is a hint; it is not a count of cycles.

A least-squares fit takes that impossible job seriously. Its square error is dominated by the early rows, where the labels are large and the channels say little, and it spends its weights trying to explain them. The paper adds that participants were never given the health index; they had to infer it from the sensed variables. The uncapped label asks them to infer it before it exists.

LiveThe cap, and why 125 sits under 1284 min▶

The piecewise-linear target holds the label flat at the cap and lets it fall only once the countdown drops below it. One recent paper sets its early-RUL threshold at 125 "based on the minimum run length of 128 cycles observed in the training data", so the model is "only asked to track the RUL once the engine approaches end of life" (Tsurkan et al., 2025). The minimum is checkable: engine 39, 128 cycles. With the cap at 125, every training engine starts on the flat part, engine 39 for its first 3 cycles and engine 69 until cycle 237.

Move the cap to 130 and engine 39's label never touches it: 127 at cycle 1 is already under. That is not a flaw. It only shows that "every engine starts flat" is a property of one cap on one subset, not of the method.

Real world

Harrow Lane's analyst (constructed, like the shop) showed the planners two charts of the same engine: the raw countdown, and the capped one. The head planner pointed at the flat part and said that was the only honest thing the old report had never told them: "not yet, and we cannot say more". The capped target matched the question they actually ask each week. It did not answer how long a healthy engine will last, and nobody pretended it did.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree4 min▶

Both values are in current use. Zhou et al. (2020): "We use piece-wise linear RUL as the target function, where the maximal RUL is 130". Tsurkan et al. (2025) use 125, tied to the 128-cycle minimum. A 2024 survey adopts the piecewise-linear label citing Heimes (2008) and sets the maximum to 125, citing Li, Ding and Sun (2018). Heimes is the paper usually credited with the idea; the cap value Heimes himself used was not read here, so it is reported second-hand only and no value is attributed to it.

The cap is also a bet about the range the model will be tested on. The PHM08 test set's true remaining lives ran from 10 to 150 cycles; the validation set's ran from 6 to 190, a widening the paper says was not announced to participants. A model capped at 125 can never say more than 125, so on any engine with more life it is early by construction. The bench's cut points run 10 to 150, so it pays that cost on some units and prints it.

The fair statement: a convention, chosen on this subset; it is a modelling choice, not a law of engines. Practitioner session 10 lets you toggle it on the full ladder.

Part 2 · covers the floor and the convention check

The floor, and what the target buys

12 min live

The floor predicts the average training life, 200.37 cycles over the 70 training engines, minus the cycles seen so far, floored at zero. It knows an engine's age and nothing else. The figure shows the three kinds of engine it meets: short-lived ones it is always late on, average ones it is close on, and long-lived ones it writes off at exactly the moment they have the most life left.

LiveReading the first three rungs4 min▶

At the canon settings, on 300 units under test: the floor prints RMSE 45.61 and a summed score of 305,167, with a bias of -12.1 (early on average) and 43 percent of units late. Rung 1, a least-squares fit on the 14 raw channels with the uncapped label, prints 30.50 and 28,557, but its bias is +15.8 and 70 percent of its calls are late, the side the score punishes harder. Rung 2, the capped label, prints 24.00 and 3,542, bias +0.8, 56 percent late.

Here is the part worth slowing down for. In the engine, rung 2 uses the same 14 channels and the same least squares as rung 1. The fitted line, scaled 0 to 1, is the learned health index session 4 builds; then it is clipped to that range and multiplied back by the cap. So the move from 30.50 to 24.00, and from 28,557 to 3,542, is the target and its clip. No new input, no new model.

Every score on this page is the PHM08 asymmetric score summed over the 300 bench units, which are held-out training engines, not the NASA test set. Practitioner session 7 opens the formula; tonight it is enough that late costs more than early.

LiveWhy the floor is the floor4 min▶

The floor is the score of knowing an engine's age and nothing else, so any model that reads the sensors has to beat it on the same cut points or the sensors added nothing. Its summed score of 305,167 is not an average of bad calls. Engines 69 (life 362) and 96 (life 336) are past the 200-cycle average at their cut points, so the floor predicts 0 to 14 cycles left when 140 to 150 remain. Those six units carry 79.5 percent of the sum, and the single worst unit (d = -142) costs 55,440 alone, 18.2 percent. A sum of exponentials is ruled by its worst few units.

Beating the floor is not automatic. At seed 7 the uncapped linear fit is worse than the floor on RMSE (40.40 against 38.06), and at seeds 1 and 7 its score is worse than the floor's. On every seed tried (2008, 1, 7, 42), the capped rung beats both. That is the strongest claim the bench supports about the target.

Real world

Harrow Lane's planners (constructed) had been using exactly the floor for years, as a rule of thumb: "an engine is good for about 200 cycles". The analyst ran it on the bench and showed them where its cost sat: not spread across the fleet, but on the two long-lived engines the rule had told them to pull while they still had most of their margin. The rule of thumb became the line every new model is drawn against, and nobody was embarrassed by it.

LiveThe convention check: 125 against 1304 min▶

The bench prints the cap-130 row on its smoothed rung (rung 3: the health index smoothed by a lag-free local line over its last 10 values). At seed 2008, cap 125 gives RMSE 23.64 and a score of 3,366; cap 130 gives 23.70 and 3,622, with the bias moving later, from +1.4 to +3.0. The RMSE barely moves; the score moves by 256. Same models, same units: only the convention changed.

Do not read a winner into that. At seeds 1 and 7, 125 does better again (24.73 against 25.13, and 24.82 against 25.39). At seed 42, 130 wins on both numbers: 21.89 and 2,453 against 22.32 and 2,562. The order of the two caps depends on which engines the seeded draw holds out. Try it in the widget above: change the seed, and watch the verdict line.

Part 3 · build-along

Rebuild the floor, the first two rungs and the other cap

18 min hands on

Open the browser console on this page (the sample and the engine are already loaded). Six steps, each with the number you should see at seed 2008. The figure is what rung 2 does to the label, box by box, so you can see where the cap enters and where it bites.

Score the floor

Rung 0 at the canon settings. You should see an RMSE of 45.61, a summed score of 305,167, a training mean life of 200.37 and 300 units under test.

consolevar r0 = IOT.ladder(IOT_SAMPLE, { only: ["mean"] }); [r0.mean.rmse.toFixed(2), Math.round(r0.mean.score), r0.mean.meanLife.toFixed(2), r0.mean.n]

Find where the floor's score lives

Score each unit on its own, sort, and add up the worst six. The worst unit costs 55,440; the top six carry 79.5 percent of the sum.

consolevar s = r0.cuts.map(function (c, i) { return IOT.phmScore(r0.mean.preds[i] - c.rul, 13, 10); }).sort(function (a, b) { return b - a; }); var tot = s.reduce(function (a, b) { return a + b; }, 0); [Math.round(s[0]), (s.slice(0, 6).reduce(function (a, b) { return a + b; }, 0) / tot * 100).toFixed(1)]

Rung 1: the uncapped target

Least squares on the 14 channels, label = life minus cycle. RMSE 30.50, score 28,557, bias +15.8, 70 percent late.

consolevar r1 = IOT.ladder(IOT_SAMPLE, { only: ["linear"] }).linear; [r1.rmse.toFixed(2), Math.round(r1.score), r1.bias.toFixed(1), Math.round(r1.lateShare * 100)]

Rung 2: the capped target

Same channels, same least squares, label capped at 125. RMSE 24.00, score 3,542, bias +0.8. The last number is the largest prediction on the bench: 125, the cap, exactly.

consolevar r2 = IOT.ladder(IOT_SAMPLE, { only: ["hi"] }).hi; [r2.rmse.toFixed(2), Math.round(r2.score), r2.bias.toFixed(1), Math.max.apply(null, r2.preds)]

Count the units the cap cannot reach

How many of the 300 units have more life left than the cap? 49 above 125 and 35 above 130. On those, a capped model is early whatever the sensors say.

console[r0.cuts.filter(function (c) { return c.rul > 125; }).length, r0.cuts.filter(function (c) { return c.rul > 130; }).length]

Run the other cap, then a second seed

The smoothed rung at 125 and at 130. Seed 2008: 23.64 / 3,366 against 23.70 / 3,622. Seed 42: 22.32 / 2,562 against 21.89 / 2,453. The winner changes with the seed; write both down.

consolevar a = IOT.ladder(IOT_SAMPLE, { only: ["smooth", "cap130Smooth"] }); var b = IOT.ladder(IOT_SAMPLE, { seed: 42, only: ["smooth", "cap130Smooth"] }); [a, b].map(function (x) { return [x.smooth.rmse.toFixed(2), Math.round(x.smooth.score), x.cap130Smooth.rmse.toFixed(2), Math.round(x.cap130Smooth.score)]; })

The habit to keep. When you change the target, change nothing else and score it beside the floor on the same cut points. Then name the convention in the write-up, with the value you did not pick printed next to it, on more than one seed.

Check yourself

Three questions

Why do many FD001 models hold the remaining-life label flat at 125 early in life?

A · Because no engine in the file has more than 125 cycles left
B · Because early in life the channels cannot separate a long remaining life from a short one, and 125 sits just under the shortest training life of 128, so every engine starts on the flat part
C · Because the NASA readme specifies 125 as the target
Engine 69 has 361 cycles left at cycle 1, so A is false, and the readme gives no target at all. The cap removes the part of the label no sensor can support; 125 is tied to the 128-cycle minimum by one paper, and other papers use 130.

At seed 2008 the smoothed rung prints 23.64 / 3,366 with cap 125 and 23.70 / 3,622 with cap 130. What follows?

A · 125 is the correct cap for FD001
B · The papers that use 130 made a mistake
C · On this bench at this seed 125 does a little better; at seed 42 130 wins on both numbers, so the cap is a modelling choice to test and report, not a law
One seed is one draw of held-out engines. At seeds 1 and 7, 125 wins again; at seed 42, 130 does (21.89 / 2,453 against 22.32 / 2,562). A convention gets printed beside its alternative, never crowned.

The floor's summed score is 305,167; the uncapped linear fit's is 28,557. Which reading is right?

A · Six units from two long-lived engines carry 79.5 percent of the floor's sum; a sum of exponentials is ruled by its worst few units, so look at the units before trusting the total
B · The floor is about ten times worse on every engine
C · A linear fit on the sensors always beats the floor
The floor's total is mostly engines 69 and 96, written off at 0 to 14 cycles while 140 to 150 remained. And C fails on the bench itself: at seed 7 the uncapped fit is worse than the floor on RMSE.
Before session 6

Homework

Thirty minutes, in the widget and the console on this page.

Sweep the cap across seeds

In the widget, run every cap from 100 to 150 at seeds 2008, 1, 7 and 42, and write down the cap with the lowest RMSE and the cap with the lowest score for each seed. Note whether they are the same cap, and whether any cap wins on every seed. Whatever you find, it is a finding about these 300 units, not about engines.

Name the floor's victims

Extend step 2 of the build-along to keep each unit's engine number with its score. Confirm that the six heaviest units belong to engines 69 and 96, and write one sentence on what the floor told the planner about each of them.

Write the cap for a label you own

Pick a time-to-event label from your own work (days to churn, weeks to stock-out, hours to a breach of a threshold). Say where your inputs stop carrying information about the time left, what cap that implies, and what value you would print beside it. Session 6 then asks what happens when the inputs themselves go missing near the end.
