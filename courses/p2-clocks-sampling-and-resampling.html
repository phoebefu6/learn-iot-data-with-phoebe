Clocks, sampling and resampling

A warehouse timestamp says when somebody did something. A sensor timestamp says when a device, on its own clock, decided to take a reading. Tonight is about that clock: how often it reads, what it can and cannot see at that rate, what happens when two devices disagree about the time, and what you change when you squeeze a signal into fewer rows or stretch it into more. Everything is built on a constructed signal, so you can see the truth beside the readings and change the settings yourself.

LiveWhy clocks come before channels2 min▶

Every later session works on readings: which channels move, how they combine into a health index, how far ahead a failure can be seen. Each of those quietly assumes that two readings with the same time on them were taken at the same moment, and that the gaps between readings are small enough to see what the machine did. Neither is guaranteed by a sensor. Both are decided by a clock you did not choose.

The streaming sessions already teach what happens when readings arrive late or out of order, and how to group them into windows. Tonight stays one step earlier, at the device: what the clock wrote down in the first place.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back3 min▶
  • Reading (sample). One value a sensor reported at one instant, or over one short interval.
  • Sampling rate. Readings per second. The gap between readings is one over the rate.
  • Half-rate limit. The fastest wave a fixed rate can show as itself: half the rate. Often called the Nyquist limit.
  • Alias. A wave faster than the limit that shows up in the readings as a slower wave that was never there.
  • Wall clock. Seconds, minutes, dates. What a planner, a calendar or a maintenance log uses.
  • Cycle. An operational cycle of the engine. FD001 counts time this way and never in seconds.
  • Skew. Two clocks disagreeing about the same instant. A fixed offset if it stays the same, drift if it grows.
  • Resampling. Moving a signal onto a different grid of times: down to fewer rows, or up to more.
Part 1 · covers sampling rate and cycle versus wall clock

Two ways to count time

8 min live

A logger reads its sensor on a fixed tick: so many readings per second, by the wall clock. A run-to-failure file like FD001 does something else. It keeps one row per engine per operational cycle and numbers the cycles 1, 2, 3, with no seconds anywhere. The figure draws both, and the place where they part. It is a general picture of a logger, not a claim about how NASA's simulation produced each cycle's values; the file does not say.

LiveSampling rate and the half-rate limit3 min▶

A logger that reads 10 times a second leaves a gap of a tenth of a second between readings. Anything the machine does inside that gap is invisible. That is obvious for a spike that comes and goes between two ticks. It is less obvious, and more dangerous, for a steady vibration that repeats faster than the readings can follow.

The rule is short. A fixed rate can show a repeating wave as itself only if the wave is slower than half the rate. At 10 readings a second the limit is 5 cycles per second (5 Hz). A 9 Hz vibration is past it, and it does not disappear: it reappears as a slower wave. Its new speed is its distance to the nearest whole multiple of the rate, so 9 Hz read at 10 a second shows as 1 Hz, and at 12 a second as 3 Hz. Part 3 lets you watch it happen.

Real world

Harrow Lane's constructed test stand reads a vibration probe and a temperature probe on the same logger, at the same rate, because that was the default. Temperature changes over minutes, so the rate is generous for it. Vibration repeats many times a second, so the same rate may be far too slow. One rate for every channel is a convenience, not a decision. The analyst's first question about any new channel is now: how fast can this thing change, and is the rate at least twice that?

LiveA cycle is not a clock3 min▶

FD001 is real public rows from NASA's simulation: the engines and their degradation are simulated with C-MAPSS, and every row is one engine at one operational cycle. Its time axis is the cycle count. There is no date, no hour and no duration column. That is fine for a model that predicts cycles left. It stops being fine the moment anyone asks for an answer on a calendar.

The planners want "weeks until this engine should come in". To get there the analyst has to multiply cycles left by an assumed number of weeks per cycle, which depends on how hard the engine is used and is not in the file. The arithmetic is trivial; the honesty is in labelling the result "cycles left, converted at an assumed utilisation" rather than presenting weeks as if the data had measured them.

Self-studyWhich clock stamped this row?2 min▶

A sensor row can carry several times and they are not the same thing. The device's own clock when it took the reading. The gateway's clock when it forwarded it. The database's clock when it stored it. A counter, like cycles, with no clock at all. Before any analysis, write down which one each time column is, and which device keeps it.

The difference between "when it happened" and "when it arrived", and what to do with readings that arrive late, is the subject of the late-data session of the streaming track. Here the question stops at the device: was its clock right?

Part 2 · covers skew between channels

Two devices, two clocks, one event

8 min live

Channels that come from different devices are stamped by different clocks. Join them on the timestamp and you are assuming those clocks agree. The figure shows a constructed case: both channels see the same step at the same true instant, but channel B's clock reads 0.3 seconds ahead, so it stamps the step 0.3 seconds later. A join on the stamps pairs the wrong readings for exactly the rows that matter most, the ones around the event.

LiveOffset: a clock that is simply wrong3 min▶

An offset is a fixed disagreement: device B is always 0.3 seconds ahead of device A. It is the easy case, because one number fixes it. Find an event both channels must have seen at the same true instant (a start, a step, a shutdown), slide one channel against the other until the event lines up, and the slide is the offset. In the constructed case that slide is 3 readings at 10 a second, so 0.3 seconds, and the build-along finds it with a dozen lines of code.

The cost of not fixing it is not spread evenly. Far from the event both channels are flat and an offset changes nothing. Around the event, which is the only place anyone is looking, the join pairs "after" on one channel with "before" on the other. Any feature built from both, a ratio, a difference, a correlation, is wrong there and right everywhere else, which is the hardest kind of error to notice.

Real world

Harrow Lane's analyst joined the constructed test stand's inlet and outlet loggers and computed outlet minus inlet. The difference spiked for a fraction of a second at every throttle step, and the planners asked whether that spike was a warning sign. It was the clocks. After she shifted one log by the lag that lined the throttle steps up, the spike vanished. The lesson she wrote on the wiki: a feature that only misbehaves at events is a clock problem until proven otherwise.

LiveDrift: a clock that runs at the wrong speed3 min▶

Drift is a clock that ticks slightly too fast or too slow, so the disagreement grows. Take a constructed clock that gains 50 parts per million: after one day it is 86,400 times 0.00005, which is 4.32 seconds ahead. One offset measured in the morning is wrong by the evening. Drift needs either regular resynchronisation of the device clocks, or an offset estimated from shared events at several points and fitted as a line through time.

Drift also hides inside a single device. If a logger's clock runs fast, its "10 readings a second" are slightly more than 10 per true second. Over a short window nobody notices. Over a long one, every rate and every duration computed from the stamps carries the same small error.

Self-studyFinding the lag from a shared event2 min▶

The build-along's lag finder slides channel B against channel A one reading at a time, from 10 readings back to 10 forward, and at each slide multiplies the overlapping readings pair by pair and averages the products. The slide with the largest average is the one where the two steps line up. It is the simplest form of cross-correlation, and it works because the event is large compared with the noise.

It fails quietly when there is no clear shared event, when the channels respond to the event with a real physical delay (heat takes time to travel; that delay is the machine, not the clock), or when the offset is larger than the range you search. Write down which event you used and why both channels must have seen it at once.

Part 3 · covers resampling up and down, and aliasing

Fewer rows, more rows, and a wave that was never there

10 min live

Resampling moves a signal onto a different grid of times. Going down to fewer rows forces a choice about what each row now means. Going up to more rows adds rows but never adds readings. The figure shows both on a constructed signal, and the widget under it lets you change the rate and the method and see the result printed.

LiveReading the widget3 min▶

It opens at 10 readings a second, instant readings, seed 2008. The 40 readings show a strongest wave at 1.00 Hz of size 0.98, and the trend reads 0.54 per second against a true 0.50. The vibration is real, but its speed is wrong, and an analyst with only these readings would report a slow oscillation that does not exist. Switch to 12 a second and the false wave moves to 3 Hz; 8 a second and it is back at 1 Hz. Switch to 100 or 20 a second and the 9 Hz wave appears as itself.

Now go back to 10 a second and change "what each reading is" to the mean over the gap. The false wave is still at 1 Hz, but its size falls to 0.11, and the trend reads 0.49. Averaging is a filter: it smooths away most of what changes faster than the gap, which is what you want from a logger that cannot read fast enough. It is not a cure. Some of the false wave survives, and at 20 a second the same averaging shrinks the real 9 Hz wave from 0.99 to 0.70.

LiveGoing down: an instant, a mean or a max3 min▶

Turning many readings into one row means choosing what the row says. The last value (or the value on the tick) keeps an instant and throws the rest away, so anything faster than the new gap can fold into a false wave. The mean keeps the level and suppresses fast change, including a real spike. The max keeps the spike and loses the level. None is right in general; each answers a different question, and the column name should say which.

Real world

Flat 4B is a constructed household whose electricity meter reports average power every 15 minutes. A kettle that runs for one fifth of a 15-minute slot shows up as one fifth of its power, spread across the whole slot. The reading is honest: it is exactly the mean it claims to be. What it cannot tell you is whether that slot held a short, strong load or a long, weak one. Practitioner session 9 reads that trace for what it does reveal.

LiveGoing up: interpolation invents2 min▶

Stretch 40 readings at 10 a second onto a grid of 100 a second with a straight line between neighbours and you get 391 rows, of which 40 were measured and 351 were drawn by the line. The figure's right panel shows what the line missed: the real signal wiggled nine times a second in between. Forward fill (repeat the last reading) invents just as many rows, as flat steps instead of slopes.

Upsampling is sometimes needed, usually to put two channels on one grid before a join. Do it last, flag the invented rows in a column of their own, and never compute a rate of change, a peak or a noise level from them as if they were readings.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree2 min▶

Practitioners split on when to resample. One camp resamples at ingest: every channel goes onto one common grid as it lands, so every later query is a simple join and nobody has to think about clocks again. The other camp stores every reading on its own stamps and resamples only inside each analysis, because the right grid and the right summary (instant, mean, max) depend on the question, and a choice made at ingest cannot be undone.

Both are defensible. Resampling early is simpler and cheaper to query, and it bakes one answer into every later result. Keeping raw readings costs storage and discipline, and it keeps the decision open. The habit that serves either camp: record, next to the data, the rate, the method and which clock stamped it, so the next analyst can tell what a row means.

Part 4 · build-along

Build a signal, read it badly, find the lag, stretch it

16 min hands on

Open the browser console on this page. The page defines a small helper, IP2, with the constructed signal and five short functions; read them in the page source if you want every line. Six steps, each with the number you should see at seed 2008. The signal is constructed: change the seed and your numbers will differ, while the shapes of the results will not.

Build the constructed truth

IP2.truth draws 4 seconds of the signal at 1,000 values a second, using the engine's seeded generator for the noise. You should see 4,000 values and a first value of 20.52.

consolevar y = IP2.truth(2008); [y.length, y[0].toFixed(2)]

Read it fast enough and check

IP2.sample keeps one value per tick; IP2.peak fits a straight line to the readings for the trend, removes it, and reports the strongest repeating wave left. At 100 a second you should see the truth come back: 9 Hz, size 1.00, trend 0.49 per second.

consolevar p = IP2.peak(IP2.sample(y, 100, "instant"), 100); [p.f, p.amp.toFixed(2), p.slope.toFixed(2)]

Read it too slowly and predict first

Before running this, write down where the 9 Hz wave should land at 12, 10 and 8 readings a second (its distance to the nearest multiple of the rate). You should see 3 Hz at size 1.07, 1 Hz at 0.98, and 1 Hz at 0.93.

console[12, 10, 8].map(function (fs) { var q = IP2.peak(IP2.sample(y, fs, "instant"), fs); return fs + " a second: " + q.f + " Hz, size " + q.amp.toFixed(2); })

Average instead of reading an instant

Same rate, 10 a second, but each reading is now the mean of the gap. The false wave stays at 1 Hz and shrinks to 0.11; the trend reads 0.49 instead of 0.54.

consolevar a = IP2.peak(IP2.sample(y, 10, "mean"), 10); var b = IP2.peak(IP2.sample(y, 10, "instant"), 10); [a.f, a.amp.toFixed(2), a.slope.toFixed(2), b.slope.toFixed(2)]

Two clocks: find the lag

IP2.pair builds two constructed channels that see one step at the same true instant, with channel B's clock 0.3 seconds ahead. Count the joined rows where they disagree about the step, then let IP2.bestLag find the slide that lines them up. You should see 3 rows and a lag of 3 readings, which at 10 a second is 0.3 seconds.

consolevar pr = IP2.pair(2008, 0.3, 10); var wrong = pr.a.filter(function (v, k) { return (v > 2.5) !== (pr.b[k] > 2.5); }).length; [wrong, IP2.bestLag(pr.a, pr.b, 10)]

Stretch it, and count what you invented

IP2.upsample draws a straight line between neighbouring readings onto a grid ten times finer. You should see 391 rows: 40 measured, 351 invented. That second number is the one to carry into any table you build this way.

consolevar u = IP2.upsample(IP2.sample(y, 10, "instant"), 10, 100); [u.values.length, u.measured, u.invented]

The habit to keep. For every time column, write three things before you use it: which device's clock stamped it, the rate it was read at, and what one row stands for (an instant, a mean, a max, or a filled-in value). If two columns come from two clocks, find a shared event and measure the offset before the first join.

Check yourself

Three questions

A 9 Hz vibration is logged 10 times a second. The readings show a slow 1 Hz wave. What happened?

A · The vibration slowed down during the recording
B · The wave is faster than half the reading rate, so it folds back as a slower wave that is not in the signal
C · The noise added a 1 Hz component
The limit at 10 readings a second is 5 Hz. A 9 Hz wave sits 1 Hz from the rate, so the readings line up along a 1 Hz wave. Every reading is genuine; the wave they trace is not.

Channel B's device clock runs 0.3 seconds ahead of channel A's, and both are read 10 times a second. What is the right first fix before joining them?

A · Find an event both channels saw, measure the slide that lines it up, shift B's stamps by it, then join
B · Resample both to one reading a second so the offset disappears inside each bucket
C · Drop the joined rows where the two channels disagree
Coarser buckets hide the offset but still split the event across buckets wrongly. Dropping the disagreeing rows deletes exactly the rows around the event, the ones anyone cares about.

You stretch 40 readings at 10 a second onto a grid of 100 a second with straight lines and get 391 rows. How many did the sensor measure?

A · All 391, since every row has a value
B · 351
C · 40; the other 351 were drawn by the line and should be flagged as filled in
Upsampling adds rows, never readings. The 351 in-between values are a guess, and the real signal may have done something quite different between ticks.

Homework

Thirty minutes, in the widget and the console on this page, plus one page of notes about your own data.

Predict, then check, the lowest honest rate

Using the half-rate rule, write down the lowest rate in the widget's list at which the 9 Hz wave should appear as itself. Then check it in the widget, on all three seeds. Your numbers will differ by seed; whether the wave appears at 9 Hz should not.

Try a smaller offset

Run IP2.pair with a skew of 0.1 and of 0.5 seconds, and IP2.bestLag on each. Does the lag finder recover both? What would it do with a skew of 1.5 seconds and a search range of 10 readings?

Write the three lines for a dataset you own

For each time column: which clock stamped it, the rate, and what a row stands for. If two columns come from two devices, name the shared event you would use to measure their offset. Next session triages channels; bring the list.
