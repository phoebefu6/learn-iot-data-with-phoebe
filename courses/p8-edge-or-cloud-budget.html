Edge or cloud: a bandwidth and latency budget

Every reading a sensor takes has to live somewhere: on the device that took it, or at the far end of a link. A warehouse team rarely asks that question, because its rows were born on a server. A sensor team asks it before the first model, with two pieces of arithmetic: how many bits a second the fleet produces against what the link can carry, and how many milliseconds a decision has against how long the round trip takes. Tonight builds both on a constructed fleet, in a calculator you can change, and ends with the short list of things any device trusted to decide must be able to do.

Part 0

Harrow Lane wires up its test cells

Harrow Lane Overhaul is a constructed engine-overhaul shop, and its fleet is NASA's 100 simulated engines. This quarter it is fitting 40 constructed sensor devices around its test cells, each reading 14 channels (the same count of moving channels FD001 ships) 10 times a second. The analyst has two questions on her desk. The platform team asks whether the raw stream fits the site's uplink. The test-cell lead asks something sharper: if a run goes wrong, can a stop be ordered within 500 ms of the reading that shows it? Every parameter tonight is constructed, chosen to make the arithmetic visible, and labelled so.

LiveWhy tonight is arithmetic, not measurement3 min▶

No real link was timed and no real device was profiled for this session. The numbers are what you get when you multiply and add a set of constructed inputs, and the engine's edgeBudget function does exactly that, out loud, so you can check every line. That is the honest shape of an edge-or-cloud decision at the planning stage: before anything is installed, the budget is arithmetic on assumptions, and the assumptions are the part to argue about.

The defaults: 40 devices, 14 channels, 10 readings a second, 32 bits a reading; a 2,000 kbit/s uplink; a summary of 224 bytes per device per minute; a 500 ms deadline; 120 ms link latency each way; 150 ms to compute in the cloud and 40 ms on the device. Change any of them in the calculator below and your numbers will differ from the prose.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back3 min▶
  • Edge. The device next to the sensor, or a small computer near it. It sees every reading.
  • Cloud. Here, simply "the far end of the link": a central place that sees what the link delivers, from every device.
  • Uplink. The connection from the site to the far end, rated in bits a second.
  • Raw stream. Every reading, as taken. Its rate is devices times channels times readings a second times bits a reading.
  • Summary. A few numbers computed on the device per channel per window (tonight: four numbers a channel, each minute) and sent instead of the readings.
  • Latency budget. The time from the reading that shows a problem to the decision that acts on it, added up step by step.
  • Deadline and slack. The deadline is how long the decision may take; the slack is the deadline minus the budget.
  • Device baseline. The capabilities a connected device should have by default, in the sense of NIST IR 8259A.
Part 1 · covers the bits

Which bytes cross the link

12 min live

Start with one device. Fourteen channels, ten readings a second each, 32 bits a reading: 4,480 bits leave it every second if every reading is shipped. Forty of them make the raw stream. The alternative keeps the readings on the device and sends four numbers per channel once a minute. The figure draws both lanes across the same link, and the copper box is what the second lane leaves behind.

LiveThe raw stream, one multiplication4 min▶

The whole bandwidth question is one line: 40 devices times 14 channels times 10 readings a second times 32 bits is 179,200 bits a second. Against a constructed 2,000 kbit/s uplink that is 9.0 percent of the link. Over a day it is 1.94 GB of readings, every one of which somebody will be asked to store, index and pay attention to.

Each factor is a decision someone made, often by default. Ten readings a second may be the logger's factory setting rather than what the channel needs; practitioner session 2 showed how to ask how fast a channel can actually change. Thirty-two bits may be the storage type rather than the sensor's real resolution; practitioner session 1 showed that FD001's exhaust temperature carries meaning at a step of 0.67 degrees Rankine. Halve any factor and the stream halves.

Real world

Harrow Lane's analyst ran the line, saw 9.0 percent, and wrote "fits, ship everything" in the platform ticket. The platform team agreed. That was the right answer to the question asked, and the test-cell lead's question was a different one: not "will the bits fit" but "will the decision be in time". A bandwidth budget can pass while the decision fails. The rest of tonight is about keeping both questions on the page.

LiveWhat the summary saves, and what it costs5 min▶

The constructed summary is four numbers per channel per minute (say the minimum, the maximum, the mean and the last reading), 4 bytes each: 14 times 4 times 4 is 224 bytes per device per minute. Forty devices send 1,195 bits a second, 0.06 percent of the link. That is a 150x cut, and you can read the 150 from the device's side too: each channel takes 600 readings a minute and sends 4 numbers about them.

The cost is not on the link; it is in next year's questions. Once the minute is summarised and the readings are dropped, nobody can re-read a two-second spike, compute a feature nobody thought of when the summary was designed, check a reading that looked wrong, or retrain on the raw signal. Practitioner session 2 showed the same loss from the other side: a mean over a window is honest about the mean and silent about the shape inside it. A per-minute summary is a window; the mechanics of windows live in learn streaming data, session 4.

Real world

Flat 4B, the constructed household of practitioner session 9, already lives with an edge summary: its electricity meter reports average power every 15 minutes, not every reading. That choice is why session 9 can infer when the flat is occupied but cannot say which appliance ran. The summary decided, years before any analyst arrived, which questions the data could answer.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree3 min▶

One camp says ship every reading while the link allows it: storage is the cheap side of the trade, the questions of next year are unknown, and a summary designed today quietly decides what can be learned tomorrow. Another camp says decide what you need and summarise at the source: every raw byte shipped is a byte to secure, store, govern and eventually delete, and a fleet of 40 devices becomes a fleet of 4,000 faster than its storage plan.

The middle positions are where the arguments get interesting. Keep a rolling buffer of raw readings on the device and ship a slice only when something asks for it: but then "kept" means "kept until it is overwritten", and the buffer's length is a new decision. Ship raw for a sample of devices and summaries for the rest: but the sample has to be chosen so it still sees the rare event. Nobody has settled this in general, because the answer depends on which questions the team expects to ask, and that is exactly what nobody knows in advance. Write down which camp your design sits in, and why.

Part 2 · covers the milliseconds and the device

The decision that must beat a deadline

12 min live

The bits fit. Now add up the time. A stop decision starts when the reading that shows the problem is taken; on average that reading is one sampling interval away, 100 ms at 10 a second. In the cloud path it then crosses the link, is computed on, and the order crosses back. In the edge path it is computed where it was taken. The figure draws both to scale against the 500 ms deadline.

LiveReading the calculator4 min▶

It opens at the constructed defaults with the summary off: 179,200 bit/s of raw readings, 9.0 percent of the link, and a cloud path of 490 ms that makes the 500 ms deadline by 10 ms. Push the link latency up two steps and watch the cloud path verdict flip from meets to misses. Now turn the summary on. The traffic on the link drops 150 times, and something less obvious happens: the far end now receives one summary a device a minute, so it can no longer make a 500 ms decision at all. Turning the summary on is not only a bandwidth choice; it moves the decision onto the device.

Then try the other sliders. Push the devices up until the raw stream no longer fits the link: the bandwidth question fails long before any deadline does. Drop the bits a reading to 16: the raw stream halves, the summary does not, and the cut falls from 150x to 75x. Drop the sample rate to 1 a second: the bits shrink, but now the wait for the next reading alone is a full second, and neither path meets a 500 ms deadline. Sampling rate is a latency decision as well as a bandwidth one.

LiveThe latency budget, line by line4 min▶

Cloud path: wait for the next reading (1,000 ms over 10 readings a second, 100 ms), cross the link (120 ms), compute (150 ms), send the order back (120 ms). Total 490 ms. Edge path: wait for the next reading (100 ms), compute on the device (40 ms). Total 140 ms. The deadline is 500 ms, so on paper both paths make it.

On paper is the trap. The cloud path's slack is 10 ms, so five extra milliseconds each way uses all of it, and the engine says the last link latency that still makes the deadline is 125 ms. Every term in the budget is a typical value, and a stop decision is needed on the bad day, not the typical one. The edge path has 360 ms of slack and no link term at all. That is the rule of thumb tonight earns: a decision that must beat a deadline lives where its data is born; a decision that can wait for the week lives wherever the whole fleet's data meets.

Real world

Harrow Lane's analyst rewrote the ticket as two lines. The stop decision runs on each device, with the threshold and the logic shipped to it, and the device reports every stop it makes. The weekly question the planners ask in every other practitioner session, which engines to pull for maintenance, has no 500 ms deadline and needs all 40 devices side by side, so it stays at the far end and runs on summaries. Same fleet, two decisions, two homes, chosen by the deadline rather than by the bandwidth.

LiveThe device baseline as a requirement list4 min▶

Once a device makes the stop decision, it is no longer a probe; it is part of the control path, and its own behaviour becomes a requirement. NIST IR 8259A gives a core baseline of six device cybersecurity capabilities, and it is careful about its role: "a default for minimally securable devices", which "does not specify how" each capability is achieved. That makes it a good spine for a requirement list, written in your own words for your own device:

  • Device Identification. Each of the 40 devices can be told apart, logically and physically, so a stop can be traced to the device that ordered it.
  • Device Configuration. The stop threshold and the summary settings can be changed, and only by someone allowed to change them.
  • Data Protection. The readings, the summaries and the stop logic are protected on the device and on the link.
  • Logical Access to Interfaces. Every way into the device, local or over the network, can be limited to who and what should use it.
  • Software Update. The decision logic can be updated, safely, on all 40 devices, which matters more now that the logic lives on them.
  • Cybersecurity State Awareness. The device can report its own state, so the far end knows when a device's stops should not be trusted.

NIST SP 800-213 frames device requirements from the device's place in a larger system, which is the frame tonight uses: the same probe needs a shorter list when it only streams, and a longer one when it decides. Neither document tells you how to build any of this, and nothing here is legal or procurement advice.

Fagan, M., Megas, K., Scarfone, K., and Smith, M. (2020). "IoT Device Cybersecurity Capability Core Baseline." NIST IR 8259A. DOI 10.6028/NIST.IR.8259A. Read at source. Part 3 · build-along

Write the calculator, then check it against the engine

18 min hands on

Open the browser console on this page (the engine is already loaded). You will write the budget in about a dozen lines, reproduce every number in the prose, and then ask the engine the one question your own calculator should be able to answer next: how thin is the slack? Six steps, each with the number you should see at the constructed defaults.

Write the raw-stream line

One function, four factors. The fleet should print 179,200 bits a second; one device on its own, 4,480.

consolefunction raw(p) { return p.devices * p.channels * p.hz * p.bits; } var fleet = { devices: 40, channels: 14, hz: 10, bits: 32 }; [raw(fleet), raw({ devices: 1, channels: 14, hz: 10, bits: 32 })]

Hold it against the link and the day

Divide by the uplink for the share it takes, and multiply by the seconds in a day (then divide by 8 bits a byte) for the volume. You should see "9.0" percent and "1.94" GB.

consolevar link = 2000 * 1000; [(raw(fleet) / link * 100).toFixed(1), (raw(fleet) * 86400 / 8 / 1e9).toFixed(2)]

Summarise on the device

Four numbers a channel, 4 bytes each, once a minute. You should see 224 bytes, "1195" bits a second for the fleet, and a cut of 150. The last number reads the same 150 from the device's side: 600 readings a channel a minute against 4 numbers.

consolevar summaryBytes = 14 * 4 * 4; var edge = fleet.devices * summaryBytes * 8 / 60; [summaryBytes, edge.toFixed(0), raw(fleet) / edge, 14 * 10 * 60 * 4 / summaryBytes]

Add up the two paths

The wait for the next reading, plus two link legs and the far-end compute for the cloud; plus only the device compute for the edge. You should see 490 and 140, with 10 and 360 ms to spare against 500.

consolefunction paths(hz, linkMs, cloudMs, edgeMs) { var wait = 1000 / hz; return { cloud: wait + 2 * linkMs + cloudMs, edge: wait + edgeMs }; } var p = paths(10, 120, 150, 40); [p.cloud, p.edge, 500 - p.cloud, 500 - p.edge]

Check your calculator against the engine

IOT.edgeBudget with no arguments runs the same constructed defaults. Every comparison should print true, and its reduction 150. If one prints false, your calculator and the engine disagree about an assumption: find which before trusting either.

consolevar b = IOT.edgeBudget(); [b.rawBps === raw(fleet), b.edgeBps === edge, b.cloudPathMs === p.cloud, b.edgePathMs === p.edge, b.reduction]

Find out how thin the slack is

Raise the link latency one millisecond at a time until the cloud path stops meeting the deadline. You should see 125: the last setting that still makes it, five milliseconds each way above the default. At 126 the cloud path is 502 ms. Constructed numbers; the habit is the point.

consolevar L = 120; while (IOT.edgeBudget({ linkLatencyMs: L + 1 }).cloudMeetsDeadline) L++; [L, IOT.edgeBudget({ linkLatencyMs: L + 1 }).cloudPathMs]

The habit to keep. For every decision a sensor fleet feeds, write two budgets side by side: the bits (does the stream fit, and what does a summary throw away) and the milliseconds (what is the deadline, and how much slack is left on a bad day). Put the decision where its deadline says it must live, and write the device's requirement list the moment a device starts deciding.

Check yourself

Three questions

The constructed raw stream is 179,200 bit/s, 9.0 percent of a 2,000 kbit/s link. What does that number settle?

Bandwidth and latency are separate budgets. The bits fit with room to spare, while the cloud path makes the deadline by only 10 ms. A design can pass the first budget and fail the second.

Summarising on the device (four numbers a channel a minute) cuts the traffic 150 times. What does it cost?

The summary is exactly as accurate as it claims; it is silent about everything it did not keep. 600 readings a channel a minute become 4 numbers, and the choice of those 4 decides which questions the data can answer next year.

The cloud path adds up to 490 ms against a 500 ms deadline. What is the right reading?

A budget built from typical values has to survive the bad day. The deadline decides where a decision lives; decisions that need the whole fleet and can wait are better made where the whole fleet's data meets.

Before session 9

Homework

Thirty minutes, half in the console on this page and half on paper.

Budget a stream you know

Pick a sensor or event stream you have worked with, or invent a plausible one and label it constructed. Run your raw() and paths() functions on it. Write one sentence for each budget: does it fit, and how much slack does the tightest decision have? If you do not know a decision's deadline, that is the finding; find out who does.

Name what the summary forgets

Design a summary for that stream (which numbers, over what window) and list three questions it could not answer a year from now. Then decide, in writing, which camp from "Where serious people disagree" your design sits in.

Write the six-line requirement list

For one device in your stream that makes or could make a decision, write one requirement per capability of the NIST IR 8259A core baseline, in your own words, and mark which ones get stricter if the device starts deciding. Session 9 moves the same question into a living room, where the device is a meter and the data is a household's day.
