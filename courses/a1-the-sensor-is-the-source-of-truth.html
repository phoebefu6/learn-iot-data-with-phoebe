Learn IoT & Industrial Data with Phoebe · Leader session 1 of 6

The sensor is the source of truth until it drifts

A warehouse row is written by a person or a system that meant it. A sensor row is written by a device that drifts, goes flat or drops out exactly when conditions are worst, and the row never says which. Harrow Lane Overhaul, a constructed engine-overhaul shop, has 100 engines' worth of readings from first cycle to failure and a planner who wants to know which engines to pull next week. Tonight is what those readings are, what they are not, and the four questions a leader puts to any sensor dataset before a model is discussed. The rows are real public rows from NASA's simulation, not real engines, and nothing here is purchasing or investment advice.

Part 0

Harrow Lane, on the morning the sensor feed arrives

Harrow Lane Overhaul is a constructed shop: a planning lead, two reliability engineers, a data analyst who came from a retail warehouse, and a hangar that can take a limited number of engines a week. Its fleet, for every session here, is the 100 turbofan engines in NASA's FD001 training set: simulated engines, each started with its own unknown wear, each run cycle by cycle until it failed. The analyst opens the file and sees what looks like a tidy fact table. It is not one, and the ways it is not are the subject of the six leader sessions.

★ What you walk out with tonight. A clear picture of what a sensor row is: a reading taken at a place in a machine, by a device with no intent, at a moment chosen by a clock. Where the 21 channels of a simulated turbofan are read, and why 7 of them never move in this data. What drift looks like across a fleet: the exhaust temperature that barely changes for seven tenths of an engine's life and then climbs. And four questions to put to any sensor dataset before anyone shows you a model. Nothing here is advice on what to buy or which machine to service.

LiveWhy the warehouse instinct misfires here4 min▶

A warehouse analyst trusts three things without naming them. A row has an author: an order was placed by a customer, an invoice raised by a clerk, a status changed by a system that was told to change it. A row has an identity: a customer id, an order id, something a person can be asked about. And a wrong row is visible: a negative quantity, a missing key, a total that does not reconcile.

A sensor row has none of the three. Its author is a device bolted to a machine, and the device meant nothing by it. Its identity is a machine and a cycle count, not a person who can be phoned. And a wrong row looks exactly like a right one: a temperature probe that has drifted two degrees writes a perfectly formatted number two degrees off, every cycle, forever. Each absence on its own is a caveat. Together they make the table a different kind of object.

Real world

Harrow Lane's analyst ran the checks she always ran: nulls, duplicates, ranges, keys. Every one passed. The file has no nulls at all, every unit has every cycle from 1 to its last, and every value sits in a plausible range. The planning lead took that as a clean bill of health. What the checks could not see is that seven columns carry no information in this operating condition and that the information in the other fourteen is concentrated in the last few tenths of each engine's life. Neither is a data-quality failure. Both change what any model can learn.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • Channel. One sensor's stream of readings. FD001 has 21 sensor channels and 3 operational settings per row.
  • Cycle. One operating cycle of the engine, one row. Time is counted in cycles, not in clock time.
  • Run to failure. A series that starts with a healthy machine and ends when it fails. The training set is all run to failure; the test set stops some time before.
  • Remaining useful life (RUL). Cycles left before failure. The target every practitioner session is built around.
  • Health index. One number for how close a machine is to its limits. In the simulation it exists, and it was deliberately not given to anyone using the data.
  • Drift. A slow movement in a reading that is not the thing you care about changing, or is, and looks the same either way.
  • Flat channel. A channel that never moves under the conditions in the data. Not broken, just silent here.
  • Constructed. Harrow Lane and Flat 4B are invented so that the truth is knowable. The engine rows are NASA's simulation; no figure describes a real engine, plant or household.
Part 1 · covers what a sensor row is

A row nobody meant to write

15 min live

The clearest way to see what a sensor row is, is to draw where it comes from. Air enters the fan and passes through two compressors, the burner and two turbines. Probes sit between the stages and read temperature, pressure and shaft speed once a cycle. The simulation degrades one stage, the high-pressure compressor, and the readings downstream move because of it. The table on the right is all that reaches the analyst.

LiveThree ways a sensor misleads without lying5 min▶

Noise. Every reading carries measurement noise. Saxena and colleagues, who built the simulation, contaminated it in stages on purpose: the degradation path was masked by a mixture of two random distributions, passed through the engine's own dynamics, and then measurement noise was added to every output channel. The result is noise that is harder to characterise than a single clean bell curve, which is the point: real data looks like that too.

Drift. A reading that moves slowly for a reason you did not plan for. Here the drift is the signal: the exhaust temperature creeps up as the compressor degrades. In a real plant it can just as easily be the probe ageing. The row cannot tell you which, and nothing in it changes when the cause does.

Silence. Seven of the 21 channels never move in FD001: the fan-inlet temperature and pressure, the bypass-duct pressure (two distinct values in 20,631 rows), the engine pressure ratio, the burner fuel-air ratio and the two demanded fan speeds. FD001 is a single operating condition at sea level, so inlet conditions and demands are constant. They are not broken; they are uninformative here, and a model that is handed them gains nothing.

LiveThe number you would want, and why you never get it4 min▶

Inside the simulation, every engine has a health index at every cycle. It is computed from operating margins: how far the engine is from fan, low-pressure and high-pressure compressor stall, and from its exhaust temperature limit. Each margin is normalised so that 1 is healthy and 0 is the limit; the stall limits were set at 15 percent and the temperature margin at about 2 percent, and whichever margin is lowest is the health index. The simulation stopped each engine when its health index reached zero.

Participants in the 2008 challenge were not given the health index. They had to infer it from the sensed variables. That is the ordinary condition of sensor work: the quantity you care about is upstream of the readings, and you only ever see its shadow in channels placed for other reasons. Practitioner session 4 builds a health index from the fourteen moving channels; tonight the point is that one exists and was withheld.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree4 min▶

The paper that defines the data says, for an engine, "an early prediction is preferred over late predictions", and builds its scoring rule to punish lateness harder. It also says, in the same section, that "in specific situations where failures may not pose life threatening situations and early predictions may instead involve significant economic burden", the preference can change and one may not prefer conservative predictions.

So the authors of the benchmark hold both positions, for different machines. A leader cannot borrow the answer. Whether a false alarm (pull the engine early, lose the remaining life) or a missed failure (the engine fails in service) is worse is a property of the asset, the operation and the people near it, and it has to be decided before anyone reads a model's accuracy. Leader session 3 is that decision.

Part 2 · covers Harrow Lane's fleet

One hundred engines, one failure each

The fleet is 100 engines and 20,631 rows. The shortest engine lasted 128 cycles and the longest 362, with a mean of 206.31. Every engine fails exactly once, at the end, and that single event is the only ground truth in the file. LiveReading three engines3 min▶

Engine 1 runs 192 cycles. Its exhaust averages 1400.85 over the first 20 and 1424.85 over the last 20. Engine 69, the longest at 362, goes from 1402.38 to 1427.58. Engine 39, the shortest at 128, starts hotter than both at 1407.14 and ends at 1426.68. The fleet mean of first-20 averages is 1402.75 and of last-20 averages 1424.95, a rise of 22.20 degrees.

Two things follow for a leader. The starting point varies by engine (the readme says each engine begins with its own unknown initial wear and manufacturing variation), so an absolute threshold that suits one engine is wrong for another. And the end-of-life signal is large and late, so a sensor that drops out under heat removes precisely the rows that matter. Leader session 2 is about that second fact.

LiveThe cost of being wrong, both ways3 min▶

Harrow Lane's planner makes one kind of decision: pull an engine now or leave it another week. A false alarm pulls an engine with life left in it: hangar time, a spare, a line that waits. A missed failure leaves an engine in service that fails there. Neither has a user id attached, and neither shows up as a bad row in any table; each shows up later as an event the data did not warn about.

The benchmark that comes with this data prices that asymmetry directly: a prediction that is too late is punished more steeply than one that is too early by the same number of cycles. Leader session 3 takes that score apart. For tonight, the habit is to ask which mistake costs more before anyone shows you an accuracy figure. This is a framework for a decision, not advice on any purchase, contract or asset.

Self-studyWhy simulated rows, and what they let you claim3 min▶

Real run-to-failure data is rare, because the point of maintenance is that machines do not run to failure. NASA's prognostics group built these sets with C-MAPSS, a turbofan simulation, so that a whole fleet could be run to the end with the truth recorded. The repository describes four sets "simulated under different combinations of operational conditions and fault modes"; FD001 is the simplest, one condition and one fault.

That makes the rows real public rows from a simulation, not real engines. Everything here about mechanisms (drift, silent channels, end-of-life concentration, dropout bias) transfers. Any specific number (a temperature, a life, a score) is a property of this simulation and is quoted as one. The second turbofan set in the same repository, generated under real flight conditions, is self-study.

Part 3 · build-along

Four questions to put to any sensor dataset

17 min hands on

No code. Four questions, asked in order, on a sensor feed your organisation already has or is about to buy into. Each has an answer that can be written down, and a blank is itself the finding. The figure is the card to keep.

Pick one sensor feed your organisation relies on

A line, a building, a fleet, a set of meters: anything where a device writes rows nobody types. Write down what decision those rows feed, in one sentence, and who makes it.

Ask where each channel is read

Not the column names: the physical places. Draw the machine as a row of boxes, as in the first figure, and put a dot where each probe sits. Mark the stage that fails most often. If nobody can draw it, the data team is modelling channels it cannot explain.

Ask which channels never move

Under the operating conditions in the file, which columns are constant or nearly so? In FD001 it is 7 of 21. Write them down with the reason, so nobody later mistakes "flat here" for "broken" or for "safe to drop everywhere".

Ask when the data goes missing

Does the feed drop out under heat, vibration, load, a full buffer, a weak link? Those are the conditions nearest failure. Write the answer as a sentence that starts "the readings go missing when...". If the honest answer is "we do not know", that is the most important line on the page.

Ask which mistake costs more

Pulling a machine too early or leaving it too long. Put each in plain terms your planner would use: lost life, downtime, a failure in service. Do not convert either to money on the page; rank them, and say who agreed to the ranking.

Commit to a guess about Harrow Lane

A model that always predicts the fleet's average life, minus the cycles already run, is the simplest thing anyone could ship. Write down whether you expect it to be late more often or early more often, and why. Leader session 3 checks the guess against the bench.

The habit to keep. When someone hands you a sensor dataset, do not ask whether it is clean. Ask where each number was read, which ones are silent here, and when the device stops writing. A file with no nulls can still be missing the end of every story.

Check yourself

Three questions

Harrow Lane's analyst reports that the sensor file passed every check: no nulls, no duplicate keys, every value in range. What is the right reading for a leader?

A · The data is ready for modelling
B · The checks found what they look for; they cannot see silent channels, drift, or where the information sits in time, and in FD001 seven channels never move and most of the exhaust rise comes in the last three tenths of life
C · The data must have been tampered with, since real data always has nulls
Warehouse checks test structure. A sensor row can be structurally perfect and still drifted, silent or concentrated in the last few tenths of a life. The simulated rows really do have no nulls; that is not evidence of tampering.

Seven of the 21 channels in FD001 never move. What should the team conclude?

A · Those sensors are broken and should be replaced
B · Those channels are useless in every dataset and can be dropped everywhere
C · Under FD001's single sea-level operating condition they carry no information; they may matter in data with several operating conditions, so drop them here and write down why
Inlet conditions and demanded speeds are constant when the engine runs at one condition. That says nothing about the probes and nothing about other subsets, which the readme describes as six conditions.

Why does it matter that 96.4 percent of readings in the last tenth of life are in the hottest fifth of all readings, against 0.8 percent in the first tenth?

A · Because any device that drops out under heat will remove the end-of-life rows first, which are the rows a remaining-life model most needs
B · Because the hot readings are errors and should be removed
C · Because it proves every engine fails at the same temperature
The hot rows are the signal, not the noise. Starting temperatures vary by engine, so no single temperature marks failure. The point is where in time the evidence sits, and therefore what missing data would cost.
Before session 2

Homework

Twenty minutes, on one sensor feed you rely on and on the guess you just wrote down.

Write the four answers for one real feed

Where each channel is read, which never move, when the feed goes missing, which mistake costs more. One line each. Mark the ones nobody could answer.

Find out what the device does when it struggles

Ask the people who maintain it: does it send nothing, send the last value again, or send a default? Each of the three makes a different kind of hole, and leader session 2 shows what the cheapest one does to a remaining-life estimate.
