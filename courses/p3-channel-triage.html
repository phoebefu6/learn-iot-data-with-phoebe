Channel triage: flat, noisy and drifting

Twenty-one sensor columns, and a model that will happily take all of them. Tonight sorts them before anything is fitted. Seven never move at all. Fourteen do, and two numbers per channel say how: how closely it tracks the cycles left before failure, and how much of its movement is cycle-to-cycle jitter. Then the trap: the two smoothest channels in the file have the weakest correlation, and a ranking by correlation alone would drop them first. Real public rows from NASA's simulation, computed in your browser.

Part 0

Harrow Lane's analyst has too many columns

Harrow Lane Overhaul is a constructed engine-overhaul shop whose fleet is NASA's 100 simulated engines. Its analyst now knows the grain of the file and where the label comes from (session 1), and how the cycle clock differs from a wall clock (session 2). Her planners want a model by Friday. Before she fits anything she does what a careful analyst does with any wide table: she sorts the columns into the ones that say nothing, the ones that mostly shake, and the ones that move with the thing she is predicting. That sort is triage, and it takes 45 minutes to do properly.

LiveWhere tonight sits2 min▶

The data is NASA's FD001 training set, data set 6 of the Prognostics Center of Excellence repository (Saxena and Goebel, 2008): 100 engines, one operating condition, one fault mode, each run from a healthy start to failure, 20,631 rows. The degradation is simulated with C-MAPSS, so every number here describes real public rows from NASA's simulation, not real engines.

Triage is not modelling. Nothing tonight predicts a remaining life. It decides what a model in session 4 is allowed to see, and it writes down why, so that a channel left out is a decision with a reason rather than a habit.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back3 min▶
  • Flat channel. A column that takes one value (or, for P15, two values a hundredth apart) across every row of FD001.
  • Cycles to failure. The label from session 1: life minus cycle. Every correlation tonight is against it.
  • r. Pearson correlation of a channel with cycles to failure, pooled over all 100 engines. Negative means the channel rises as failure nears; positive means it falls.
  • Noise share. Within-engine cycle-to-cycle spread over the channel's total spread. Lower means a smoother trend.
  • Drift. A channel's slow movement across an engine's life, here because the engine is wearing.
  • Triage. Sorting columns by what they can carry before any model sees them, and recording the reason for each.
Part 1 · covers flat channels

Seven channels that never move, and why

10 min live

Computed on all 20,631 training rows, seven of the 21 sensor channels never move: T2, P2, P15, epr, farB, Nf_dmd and PCNfR_dmd. Operational setting 3 is constant at 100.0 as well. None of this means a probe failed. The figure places each flat channel where it reads, and sorts the seven by the reason they are flat in this one subset.

LiveThree reasons a channel is flat4 min▶

It reads the operating condition. T2 and P2 sit at the fan inlet and P15 in the bypass duct. FD001 runs at one condition, sea level, so the air arriving at the engine never changes, and neither do they. P15 takes two values, 21.60 and 21.61: a hundredth of a psia is resolution, not information.

It reads a demand, not a response. Nf_dmd and PCNfR_dmd are the demanded fan speeds. They record what the engine was asked to do, and in one fixed condition it is always asked the same thing. The measured speeds, Nf and NRf, do move.

It takes one printed value. epr reads 1.3 and farB 0.03 on every row. Whatever these do inside the simulation, it happens below the precision the file prints, so for a model they carry nothing.

Real world

A Harrow Lane planner (constructed, like the shop) asked for an alert on inlet temperature, "because heat is how engines die". On FD001 the alert would never fire: T2 is the same number on every row. Worse, a dashboard tile that never changes colour reads as reassurance. The analyst wrote one line in the triage log instead: "T2 flat, reads the operating condition, which is fixed in this subset; no alert can be built on it here."

LiveFlat describes the condition, not the probe3 min▶

Dropping the seven from an FD001 model is right. Writing "T2 is useless" in a shared feature list is not. The flat list is a fact about one operating condition. In the six-condition subsets (FD002 and FD004) the inlet readings may well matter, and a habit learned here would delete them there. Those subsets are not covered on any page; the point is only that the reason travels with the decision.

The shipped sample follows that rule: the seven are not shipped, and they are listed inside it with their symbols and values, so the fact is never separated from the data.

Self-studyWhy a flat channel breaks the arithmetic3 min▶

A correlation divides the shared movement of two columns by the product of their spreads. A flat column has zero spread, so its correlation with anything is zero divided by zero: not "weak", undefined. Standardising it (subtract the mean, divide by the spread) divides by zero too. Many pipelines quietly turn that into a column of missing values or a column of zeros, and neither announces itself.

That is the practical reason to triage flat channels first and by name, before any step that divides by a spread runs.

Part 2 · covers correlation and noise

Two lenses: r with life, and noise share

9 min live

For each of the fourteen channels that move, two numbers, both computed on all 100 engines. r asks how well one straight line, shared by the whole fleet, describes the channel against cycles to failure. Noise share asks how much of the channel's spread is cycle-to-cycle jitter. The figure draws what each one actually measures, because each is blind to something the other sees.

LiveReading r3 min▶

Sorted by size, the strongest three are Ps30 (HPC outlet static pressure, -0.696), T50 (LPT outlet temperature, -0.679) and phi (fuel flow over Ps30, +0.672). The sign is a direction, not a quality: Ps30 and T50 rise as failure nears, phi, P30, W31 and W32 fall. At the bottom sit Nc (-0.390) and NRc (-0.307), the core shaft read physically and corrected.

No channel gets near 1, and the figure shows one reason. Most of T50's rise comes late in life, so the cloud is bent and a straight line can only cut through it. Two more reasons come from the readme: each engine starts with its own unknown initial wear and manufacturing variation, so engines sit at different heights; and the data carries sensor noise. A pooled r is reduced by all three at once and cannot say which.

LiveReading noise share3 min▶

Noise share is the cycle-to-cycle spread inside each engine (the spread of first differences, divided by the square root of 2) over the channel's total spread. T30 is the noisiest at 0.65, then htBleed 0.61 and T24 0.60: well over half of what these channels do from one cycle to the next is jitter. The smoothest are NRc at 0.17 and Nc at 0.19, then Ps30 at 0.38.

A high noise share does not make a channel useless. T24 still correlates at -0.606. It means a model reading one cycle at a time will be shaken by it, which is the case for smoothing in session 4 and for drawing the trace before trusting any single reading.

Real world

Harrow Lane's analyst (constructed) ranked the fourteen by r, kept the top ten, and had a draft feature list in five minutes. Then she sorted the same table by noise share and saw the two channels she had just cut, Nc and NRc, sitting at the top as the smoothest in the file. Weak r with a smooth trace is not a quiet channel. It is a channel doing something a straight line does not describe. She put both back on a "look before deciding" list.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree3 min▶

Should triage rank by one number, or read several? A single ranking is fast, repeatable and easy to defend in a review. Several numbers catch what one hides, but leave a judgement call that two analysts can make differently.

The same argument runs through the PHM08 paper itself, at the scoring end rather than the input end. Saxena, Goebel, Simon and Eklund (2008) define one summed score for the challenge, and also argue for weighting short true remaining lives more and for adding a correlation metric, because a summed score can hide uneven performance across units. Every later session prints several numbers side by side and none is crowned; triage tonight does the same with r and noise share. Which channels a model should finally keep is not settled here, and session 4 shows one way to stop having to choose.

Part 3 · covers drift across life

Drift: what the engine does between birth and failure

9 min live

r squeezes a whole life into one number. Drift is the shape it squeezed. Cut each engine's life into ten equal tenths and average T50 across the fleet: 1402.57 degrees Rankine in the first tenth, 1425.04 in the last, and most of the rise in the last three tenths. Then do the same for the two channels at the bottom of the ranking, and they stop looking like nothing.

LiveT50 by tenth of life3 min▶

Across all 100 engines and 20,631 rows, the fleet mean of T50 by tenth of life reads 1402.57, 1402.75, 1403.41, 1404.35, 1405.48, 1406.96, 1409.16, 1412.57, 1417.50 and 1425.04 degrees Rankine. The first half of life moves less than three degrees; the last three tenths carry most of the climb. Averaged over each engine's first 20 cycles the fleet reads 1402.75, over its last 20 cycles 1424.95: a rise of 22.20.

That shape is drift: slow, one-way, tied to wear. It is also why the cloud in the Part 2 figure bends, and why a straight-line r for the best temperature channel in the file stops at -0.679. Set the widget to s4 · T50 and it prints these same tenths.

Real world

Harrow Lane's reliability engineer (constructed) proposed a fixed T50 alarm level for the whole fleet. The analyst pulled two engines. Engine 1 averages 1400.85 over its first 20 cycles and 1424.85 over its last 20; engine 39, the shortest-lived in the file, starts at 1407.14 and ends at 1426.68. Engine 39 is born hotter. A fixed level measures where an engine started as much as how far it has drifted, and the analyst's note said so: "drift is a change from the engine's own start, not a height".

LiveNc and NRc: look before you rank3 min▶

Nc and NRc carry the weakest r of the fourteen (-0.390 and -0.307) and the lowest noise share (0.19 and 0.17). Those two facts together say the channels move a great deal relative to their jitter, but not along one straight line that the whole fleet shares. What shape the movement takes is a question for the traces, not for the table.

Set the widget to s9 · Nc, then s14 · NRc. It prints the fleet mean by tenth of life and counts the engines whose last 20 cycles average below their first 20. Compare that count with T50's. When engines move in opposite directions near the end, a pooled r adds the two directions together and shrinks, however clearly each engine is moving. Why the core speed goes one way in some simulated engines and the other way in others is not something the file or its readme states; treat it as an open question, and treat "weak r" as a reason to look, never as a reason to drop.

Self-studyPhysical drift is not pipeline drift3 min▶

Tonight's drift is the thing a remaining-life model exists to read: the engine changing, measured faithfully. A pipeline drift monitor watches for the opposite: a table's distribution changing because something upstream changed, which is usually a fault to be fixed. Both use the same word and sometimes the same statistics, and a monitor tuned to alarm on "drift" would alarm on every engine approaching failure, which is to say on the signal.

Pipeline data quality, freshness, volume and distribution-drift monitors are taught in learn data observability, builder session 5. They are linked, not retaught. Here the drift is in the physical sensor.

Part 4 · build-along

Triage the fourteen in your own console

15 min hands on

Open the browser console on this page (the sample and the engine are already loaded). Six steps, each with what you should see. By step 3 you have reproduced the full triage table; by step 6 you have looked at the drift behind the two numbers at its bottom. The figure is the table you are about to print, drawn.

Run the triage

IOT.triage decodes all 100 engines and returns two lists: the fourteen channels that move, each with r and noise share, and the seven flat ones carried inside the sample. You should see 14 and 7.

consolevar tri = IOT.triage(IOT_SAMPLE); [tri.kept.length, tri.flat.length]

Read the flat seven

Each flat channel with its symbol, the number of distinct values in the original file, and its range. Every one takes a single value except P15, which takes two: 21.60 and 21.61. Copy the list into your triage log with the reason for each, from Part 1.

consoletri.flat.map(function (f) { return f.ch + " " + f.sym + ": " + f.distinct + " value(s), " + f.min + " to " + f.max; })

Print the triage table

One line per moving channel: channel, symbol, r to three places, noise share to two. Check each line against the figure above and the widget's table; they are the same computation. s4 · T50 should read -0.679 and 0.45.

consoletri.kept.map(function (c) { return c.ch + " " + c.sym + " " + c.r.toFixed(3) + " " + c.noiseShare.toFixed(2); })

Rank by the size of r

Sort a copy by the absolute value of r. You should see Ps30, T50 and phi first, and Nc then NRc last. If you stopped here, these are the two you would cut.

consoletri.kept.slice().sort(function (a, b) { return Math.abs(b.r) - Math.abs(a.r); }).map(function (c) { return c.sym; }).join(" ")

Rank by noise share

Now sort the same list from smoothest to noisiest. NRc and Nc come first, Ps30 third; T24, htBleed and T30 come last. The two lenses disagree most at exactly the two channels the first ranking would have dropped.

consoletri.kept.slice().sort(function (a, b) { return a.noiseShare - b.noiseShare; }).map(function (c) { return c.sym + " " + c.noiseShare.toFixed(2); }).join(", ")

Look at the drift behind the numbers

For a channel, average each engine's first 20 and last 20 cycles, take the fleet means, and count the engines that end lower than they start. For T50 you should see 1402.75 and 1424.95, the rise of 22.20. Then read the third number for T50, Nc and NRc side by side, and compare it with what the widget prints for the same channels.

consolevar units = IOT.decode(IOT_SAMPLE); function drift(ch) { var k = IOT_SAMPLE.channels.indexOf(ch), a = 0, b = 0, lower = 0; units.forEach(function (u) { var f = 0, l = 0; for (var t = 0; t < 20; t++) { f += u.rows[t][k]; l += u.rows[u.n - 20 + t][k]; } a += f / 20; b += l / 20; if (l < f) lower++; }); return [(a / units.length).toFixed(2), (b / units.length).toFixed(2), lower]; } [drift("s4"), drift("s9"), drift("s14")]

The habit to keep. Triage every new sensor table in three passes and log a reason for each column: flat (and why: condition, demand or precision), noisy (by noise share), and moving with the target (by r). Then look at the trace of anything where the two numbers disagree before you drop it. A column cut on one number is a decision you cannot explain later.

Check yourself

Three questions

Nf_dmd, the demanded fan speed, never moves across the FD001 training rows. What is the best explanation?

A · The probe failed early in every engine
B · It records a command, not the engine's response, and in FD001's one fixed operating condition the command never changes
C · The shipped sample's quantisation rounded its movement away
A demanded speed is a setpoint. The measured fan speeds, Nf and NRf, do move. And the flat seven were flat in the original file: they are listed, not shipped, so quantisation never touched them.

Nc (s9) has r -0.390 and noise share 0.19. Ps30 (s11) has r -0.696 and noise share 0.38. What follows?

A · Drop Nc: it has the second weakest r of the fourteen
B · Nc is the noisier channel of the two
C · Nc moves smoothly but not along one straight line the whole fleet shares, so r undersells it; look at its drift before deciding
A lower noise share means a smoother trend, so Nc is the smoother of the two. A weak pooled r with a smooth trace is the signature of movement a single straight line cannot describe, such as engines starting at different levels or moving in different directions near the end.

Tonight's T50 drift (fleet mean 1402.57 in the first tenth of life, 1425.04 in the last) and a pipeline drift alert share a word. What is tonight's drift?

A · The engine wearing, read faithfully through a probe: the signal a remaining-life model is meant to learn from
B · A table's distribution shifting because something upstream in the pipeline changed
C · Two channels' clocks slipping apart, so their readings no longer line up
B is pipeline drift, taught in the data observability sessions; C is clock skew, from session 2. Here the drift is in the physical sensor, and a monitor that alarmed on it would alarm on the very thing being predicted.
Before session 4

Homework

Thirty minutes, in the console on this page and then on a table of your own.

Read three engines, not the fleet

Using the drift function from step 6 as a pattern, print the first-20 and last-20 averages of Nc for engines 1, 39 and 69 only (units[0], units[38], units[68]). Write one sentence on what a single pooled r of -0.390 hides about these three.

Try a third lens

For each of the fourteen channels, divide the fleet's average rise (last 20 minus first 20) by the channel's cycle-to-cycle jitter. Rank by that. Which channels move up compared with the r ranking, and which move down? Bring the list to session 4, where the channels are combined into one number.

Triage a table you own

Pick any wide table at work. Log every column as flat (with the reason), noisy, or moving with the outcome you care about, and mark any column where two measures disagree. Count how many of your "drop" decisions rested on one number.
