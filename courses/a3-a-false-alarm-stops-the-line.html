A false alarm stops the line

A warehouse model is wrong in one currency: a forecast too high or too low, priced the same either way. A remaining-life estimate is wrong in two. Too early, and an engine with life left is pulled, a spare is sent and the line waits. Too late, and the engine fails in service. Tonight Harrow Lane Overhaul, a constructed engine-overhaul shop, takes apart the score published with its data, finds that it is a business choice about those two mistakes, and watches one summed number hide how unevenly 300 estimates went wrong. The rows are real public rows from NASA's simulation, not real engines.

Part 0

Harrow Lane, the morning the model review lands

Harrow Lane Overhaul is a constructed shop, and its fleet is the 100 turbofan engines in NASA's FD001 training set: simulated engines, each run cycle by cycle until it failed, the degradation itself simulated (C-MAPSS). In leader session 2 a cleaning step pushed the shop's estimates late and one number noticed while the other barely moved. Tonight the planning lead asks the question that sat underneath: when an estimate is wrong, which way is worse, who decided, and does the number on the review slide even know?

★ What you walk out with tonight. A clear picture of the two ways a remaining-life estimate goes wrong and what each one costs on a shop floor. A reading of the score published with this data: it punishes a late estimate more steeply than an early one, and that lean is a choice its authors made for engines, not a law. A measured case of one summed number hiding uneven units: the simplest baseline's 305,167, most of it from two long-lived engines. And a one-line review statement, written before anyone shows you a model, that says which mistake costs more and which numbers will show it.

LiveWhy "the most accurate model" misfires here3 min▶

A warehouse forecast that is 50 units high and one that is 50 units low usually cost about the same, so a square-error figure that treats them alike is a fair summary. Most dashboards are built on that habit: one accuracy number, smaller is better, no direction.

A remaining-life estimate has a direction, and the two directions buy different events. An estimate that says "fails sooner than it will" triggers a pull with life left: a false alarm. An estimate that says "fails later than it will" leaves an engine in service past its end: a miss. The same number of cycles wrong can mean an idle hangar bay or a failure in service, and no directionless accuracy figure can tell you which one you bought.

Real world

Harrow Lane's analyst (constructed, like the shop) opens the review with "the new model is 23.64 cycles off on average, down from 45.61". The planning lead asks two things the slide does not answer: off in which direction, and on which engines? The rest of the session is those two questions.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • False alarm. An estimate that says the engine will fail sooner than it does. It leads to a pull with life left: lost life, a spare sent, a line that waits.
  • Missed failure. An estimate that says the engine will last longer than it does. It leaves the engine in service past its end.
  • d. The estimated remaining life minus the true remaining life, in cycles. Below zero is early; zero or above is late.
  • Asymmetric score. The scoring rule published with this data in 2008: each unit's miss is turned into a cost that grows exponentially, and faster on the late side.
  • Summed score. The unit costs added over every unit under test. Harrow Lane's bench sums 300 of them.
  • Bias. The mean of d across the units. Positive means the estimates lean late.
  • Late share. The share of units whose estimate lands late.
  • Unit under test. One engine at one cut point. Harrow Lane's bench has 300: 10 seeded cut points on each of 30 held-out engines, not the NASA test set.
  • Constructed. Harrow Lane is invented so that the truth is knowable. The engine rows are NASA's simulation; no figure describes a real engine or plant.
Part 1 · covers early versus late cost and the score as a business choice

Two mistakes, two prices

13 min live

The cleanest way to see the asymmetry is to draw one engine's life and put the estimate on each side of its true failure. Then draw how the published score turns each side into a cost. The two halves of the figure are the same idea twice: what the mistake does on the floor, and how the score prices it.

LiveWhat each mistake buys on the floor5 min▶

A false alarm stops the line. The estimate says the engine is close to its end, so the planner pulls it. The hangar bay is used, a spare engine goes out, the work queue behind it waits, and the inspection finds an engine with life left. That life is thrown away: nobody gets those cycles back by putting it on the stand early. It is a cost, but it is a planned cost, paid in a workshop.

A missed failure breaks an engine. The estimate says there is plenty left, so the engine stays in service and fails there: unplanned, wherever it happens to be, with whatever follows. For an engine the paper that published this data is explicit about which it prefers: an early prediction over a late one. That preference is the whole reason its score is lopsided.

Real world

Harrow Lane's planning lead (constructed) keeps two lists on the wall. One is every engine pulled in the last quarter that the strip-down showed had plenty of life left. The other is every engine that came in on a failure nobody planned for. The first list is longer. The second is the one the shop's customers remember, and the one the reliability engineer loses sleep over. Neither list has a number for cost on it, and neither needs one to show that they are different kinds of bad.

LiveHow the published score prices the two4 min▶

For each unit, take d: the estimated remaining life minus the true one. If d is below zero the estimate was early, and the unit's cost is e raised to the power of the size of d over 13, minus 1. If d is zero or above the estimate was late, and the cost is e to the power of d over 10, minus 1. Add the costs over every unit and you have the score. Smaller is better; a perfect estimate costs 0.

Two features matter to a leader. The penalty "grows exponentially with increasing error", in the paper's words, so a few large misses outweigh many small ones. And the late side divides by a smaller number, so it climbs faster: in the paper's own plot of the curve, 50 cycles early costs about 46 and 50 cycles late about 147. The authors state the intent plainly: "late predictions were more heavily penalized than early predictions".

One caution before you quote the formula anywhere. The paper's printed equation pairs the two constants the other way round from its plotted curve and its prose. Harrow Lane's bench follows the plot and the prose; practitioner session 7 reads the equation and the figure side by side. Tonight it is enough to know the disagreement exists, so that you check which way any score you are shown actually leans.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree4 min▶

Which way should the score lean? The same section of the same paper that punishes late harder also says that "in specific situations where failures may not pose life threatening situations", early predictions "may instead involve significant economic burden", and the preference can be tuned. So the benchmark's own authors hold both positions, for different machines. A plant where a missed failure means a slower line and a false alarm means a full stop might sensibly lean the other way.

Which way does this score lean on paper? The printed equation and the plotted curve disagree, as above. Every later paper you meet may have copied either one. Practitioner session 7 is where that is read in full; it is not settled here.

One number or several? The paper ranked the 2008 challenge on one summed score and, in the same text, argued for more than one number. Part 2 shows why. Harrow Lane's bench prints four and crowns none.

Part 2 · covers one number hiding uneven units

One number, three hundred uneven mistakes

12 min live

The simplest model anyone could ship predicts the fleet's average life minus the cycles already run. On Harrow Lane's bench (300 cut points on 30 held-out engines, not the NASA test set) its summed score is 305,167. That one number is mostly two engines. LiveWhy the always-average model scores 305,1674 min▶

The baseline knows one thing: the 70 training engines lived 200.37 cycles on average. At every cut point it predicts 200.37 minus the cycles already run, and never less than zero. For an ordinary engine that is a rough but bounded guess. For an engine already past cycle 200 at its cut point it predicts zero: fails now.

Two of the 30 held-out engines are exactly that case. Engine 69 is the longest in FD001 at 362 cycles and engine 96 lives 336. At their cut points the baseline predicts 0 to 14 cycles left when 140 to 150 remain. Those are false alarms of enormous size, and the exponential charges them accordingly: the single worst unit (142 cycles early) costs 55,440 on its own, 18.2 percent of the sum, and six units from those two engines carry 79.5 percent. The other 294 units together carry the rest.

That also settles the guess leader session 1 asked you to write down. Across the 300 units the baseline lands late on 43 percent and its mean error is 12.1 cycles early. It is an early-leaning model whose worst failures are false alarms, and even on the side the score treats gently, a large enough miss dominates everything.

LiveThe ladder through two lenses4 min▶

Harrow Lane's bench climbs four rungs, each fitted on the 70 training engines and tested on the same 300 units. Read them with the square error and the summed score side by side:

  • Rung 0, the mean-life baseline. RMSE 45.61, score 305,167, mean error 12.1 cycles early, 43 percent late.
  • Rung 1, a linear fit on 14 raw channels. RMSE 30.50, score 28,557, mean error 15.8 cycles late, 70 percent late. Better on both numbers, and it has swapped an early lean for a late one.
  • Rung 2, a health index with a capped target. RMSE 24.00, score 3,542, mean error +0.8, 56 percent late. The cap of 125 cycles is a convention chosen on this subset; 130 is also in use.
  • Rung 3, plus lag-free smoothing. RMSE 23.64, score 3,366, mean error +1.4, 56 percent late. The smoothing gain is small, 0.36 of RMSE, and a trailing mean over the same window lags and makes both numbers worse (24.88 and 4,171). Cap 130 instead of 125 gives 23.70 and 3,622.

On the four main rungs the two lenses agree on the order. They disagree on the size of each step and on what a step was made of. RMSE says rung 1 closed most of the distance to rung 3 (30.50 against 23.64); the score says rung 1 is still more than eight times worse (28,557 against 3,366). Neither says which way the misses lean; only the bias and the late share do.

Real world

Harrow Lane's analyst (constructed) had planned to report "score per unit" as the headline because it reads like an average: 1,017.22 for the baseline. The reliability engineer points at the widget. For the baseline, the median unit costs a small fraction of that and most units cost less than the mean, because six units carry most of the sum. An average of a sum of exponentials describes almost no unit in it.

Self-studyWhat the paper's authors asked for instead3 min▶

Saxena and colleagues, who published the data and the score, did not argue that one summed number is enough. In the same section that defines the score they argue for weighting units with a short true remaining life more heavily, and for a correlation measure beside it, because a summed score can hide uneven performance across units. Tonight's baseline is a clean example of the unevenness they meant.

For a leader the practical version is short: ask for the bias, the late share and the worst few units beside any summed score, and ask whether the worst units are early or late. Measuring a detector's precision and recall is a different job, taught in learn anomaly detection, measuring detection; RMSE and backtesting live in learn time-series forecasting, backtesting and metrics. Neither is retaught here.

Part 3 · build-along

Set the lean before you read the model

17 min hands on

No code. A decision exercise, on Harrow Lane's printed numbers first and then on an asset of your own: rank the two mistakes, set the lean and say who signed it, then decide which numbers the review will carry. The figure is the sheet to fill in.

Rank Harrow Lane's two mistakes

Harrow Lane overhauls engines, and a failure in service is the event its customers remember. Write one sentence: which mistake costs the shop more, a pull with life left or a failure in service, and why. Do not reach for money; rank them in the planner's words.

Check the lean against the published one

The published score divides a late miss by 10 and an early one by 13, so late costs more: about 147 against about 46 at 50 cycles. Does that match your ranking? If it does, write "we adopt the published lean". If it does not, write which way you would tilt it and say that the score you report is no longer the published one.

Read the four rungs with the lean in hand

Use the ladder in the figure. Rung 1 improves on the baseline by every number, yet 70 percent of its estimates land late against 43 percent for the baseline. Under a lean that punishes late harder, which rung would you be willing to put in front of the planner, and what would you want to see before you did?

Ask for the worst units

In the widget, pick the baseline and then rung 3. Note how much of each sum the worst unit and the worst six carry, and whether those units are early or late. Write one line: "our score is ruled by ..." If the answer is two engines, the next question is what is unusual about them; here, they simply lived far longer than the average.

Name the numbers your review will carry

At least the square error, the summed score, the bias and the late share, side by side, with the worst few units listed. Write down which one, if any, decides between two models, and why. The paper that published the score argued for more than one number; leading with only one is a decision too.

Write the review line for an asset of your own

"For this asset a ... costs more than a ..., so our score leans ...; agreed by ...; we report RMSE, the summed score, the bias, the late share and the worst units." One line, signed. A blank in it is the finding to take to the next model review.

The habit to keep. Before anyone shows you how accurate a model is, decide which way being wrong is worse and say who decided. Then ask for the direction of the misses and the worst few units, never just one summed number. A score that leans is a business choice someone already made; make sure it was yours.

Check yourself

Three questions

Harrow Lane's planner asks the data team which number should lead the monthly model review. What is the right answer?

A · RMSE, because it is the standard accuracy figure and treats every miss alike
B · The summed asymmetric score, because it is the one published with the data
C · First decide which mistake costs this shop more and who agreed; then report RMSE, the summed score, the bias and the late share side by side, with the worst units
RMSE has no direction, and a summed score is ruled by its worst few units. The paper that published the score argued for more than one number. The lean is a business decision that has to be made before any of the numbers can be read.

The mean-life baseline scores 305,167 on Harrow Lane's 300 units under test. What explains most of that number?

A · Six units from two long-lived engines, 69 and 96, where the baseline predicts 0 to 14 cycles left when 140 to 150 remain; they carry 79.5 percent of the sum
B · Every unit being about 1,017 wrong, since that is the score per unit
C · Late estimates on most units, since the score punishes late harder
The baseline puts every engine's failure at the fleet average of 200.37 cycles, so engines that outlive the average get huge false alarms. The baseline is late on only 43 percent of units. A sum of exponentials is ruled by its worst few units, so the per-unit average describes almost none of them.

At 50 cycles off, the published score charges about 46 for an early estimate and about 147 for a late one. How should a leader read that lean?

A · As a law of engines: late is always about three times worse than early
B · As a choice the paper made for engines, where a failure in service is the feared event; the same paper says early can carry a significant economic burden elsewhere, so each organisation sets its own lean
C · As a printing error, since the paper's printed equation disagrees with its figure
The authors state their preference and say it can be tuned. The printed equation and the figure do disagree on which constant goes with which side; practitioner session 7 reads that in full, and it is a reason to check any score you are shown, not a reason to dismiss the lean.
Before session 4

Homework

Twenty minutes, on the review line you wrote tonight.

Get the review line signed

Take the line to whoever owns the decision the model feeds: the planner, the operations lead, the reliability owner. Ask them to rank the two mistakes in their own words. If their ranking differs from yours, write both down; the disagreement is the useful part.

Ask for the worst five units

For the last model your team reported on, ask for the five units or cases with the biggest misses, and whether each was early or late. Compare them with the headline number. If the team cannot produce them, that is the finding.

Check which way your own score leans

If your team uses any asymmetric or weighted score, find where its constants come from and which side each applies to, from the code rather than the slide. Leader session 4 moves from engines to homes, where the cost of being wrong is a person's privacy.
