Missing is never random

A warehouse analyst meets a blank cell and drops the row: a few rows fewer, the same answer. A sensor goes quiet for a reason, and the commonest reason is that conditions got bad: heat, load, vibration, the last weeks of a machine's life. Tonight Harrow Lane Overhaul, a constructed engine-overhaul shop, plants exactly that kind of dropout in its fleet's readings, drops the rows with gaps the way any tidy pipeline would, and watches its failing engines start to look healthier. The rows are real public rows from NASA's simulation, not real engines.

Part 0

Harrow Lane, the week the probe started going quiet

Harrow Lane Overhaul is a constructed shop, and its fleet is the 100 turbofan engines in NASA's FD001 training set: simulated engines, each run cycle by cycle until it failed. In leader session 1 the file had no nulls at all. Tonight the shop asks what happens when it does: when the exhaust probe stops reporting on its hottest cycles, as struggling devices do, and the pipeline quietly drops every row with a gap before the model ever sees it.

Live = presented in the 45 minutes Self-study = the full depth, after class 🔍 Every diagram zooms

★ What you walk out with tonight. One idea from statistics, at a leader's depth: you may ignore why a reading is missing only under conditions, and a sensor that quits when it runs hot breaks them. A picture of where the gaps land in a machine's life: almost none early, most of them at the end. A measured result on Harrow Lane's bench: when the hottest readings vanish and the gappy rows are dropped, the error a data team usually reports barely moves while the cost of being late jumps. And three questions to put to every gap in a feed you own.

LiveWhy "drop the blanks" misfires here3 min▶

In a warehouse, a blank is usually somebody's slip: a field not filled, an import that skipped a column. Slips happen to orders of every size and every customer, so dropping those rows leaves a smaller table that still looks like the business. That instinct is sound there, and it is why "drop rows with nulls" is the first line of so many cleaning scripts.

A sensor does not slip at random. It goes quiet when its link is saturated, when its housing overheats, when the machine shakes hardest, when the power dips. Those are not ordinary moments in the machine's life; they cluster where the machine is struggling. Drop those rows and you have not removed noise, you have removed the end of the story, and the model will read what is left as a calmer machine than the one on the stand.

Real world

Harrow Lane's analyst (constructed, like the shop) reports that the new exhaust feed is "about 85 percent complete, and the cleaning step handles the rest". The planning lead asks the question tonight is about: which 15 percent? The answer, once someone plots it, is that almost all of the missing cycles sit in the last few weeks of each engine's life, the weeks the planner actually has to decide about.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • Missingness. The pattern of which readings are absent, treated as data in its own right: when, where, and next to what.
  • Ignorable. A gap you may analyse around without modelling why it happened. Rubin's 1976 paper sets out when that is allowed.
  • Missing completely at random. The gap has nothing to do with the engine or the reading: a link hiccup that strikes any cycle alike.
  • Missing at random. The gap depends only on something you did record, such as a logged test mode, so you can account for it.
  • Missing not at random. The gap depends on the value that went missing: the probe quits because the reading is hot. The three names are the shorthand of later texts.
  • Dropout. A device that stops writing for a while and then resumes. Tonight's dropout is planted, so its rule is known.
  • Deletion. Dropping every row with a gap before modelling. Also called complete-case analysis. The cheapest fix, and the one measured tonight.
  • Constructed. Harrow Lane is invented so that the truth is knowable. The engine rows are NASA's simulation; no figure describes a real engine or plant.
Part 1 · covers when a gap may be ignored

Why it is missing decides what you may do

11 min live

The same probe can lose the same reading for three different reasons, and the reason, not the size of the hole, decides whether the rows you still have tell the truth. The figure draws the three: what decides whether a reading arrives, and what that means for the analyst who never sees the missing value.

LiveRubin's question, in a leader's words4 min▶

In 1976 Donald Rubin asked a question every data team answers by habit without noticing: when is it acceptable to analyse the values you have and simply ignore the process that made the others go missing? His answer, in the abstract of the paper, is that it is acceptable only under conditions, and he described the ones he gave as "the weakest general conditions" under which ignoring that process is appropriate. For the usual kinds of estimate, the core one is that the data are missing at random.

For a leader, the translation is one sentence: dropping the gaps is a claim about why they happened. If the gaps strike any cycle alike, or depend only on something you recorded and can account for, the rows you keep still describe the machine. If the gaps depend on the very reading that went missing, no amount of careful analysis of the surviving rows recovers what was lost, because the survivors are a filtered sample: the filter is the thing you were trying to measure.

LiveThree reasons a reading vanishes, on a real feed4 min▶

Completely at random. A radio link drops a packet now and then, regardless of what the engine is doing. Rare in practice, because even a link is more likely to struggle under heat or vibration, but it is the case most cleaning scripts silently assume.

At random, given what you hold. The feed is switched off during a logged test procedure, and the log is in your data. The gap is explainable from a column you have, so you can account for it: compare like with like, or weight the rows you kept.

Not at random. The probe's electronics cut out when the exhaust runs hot, so the hot readings are exactly the ones that never arrive. Nothing in the surviving rows says so. A sensor that stops reporting at the worst moment is the default assumption a leader should make about any device until someone shows otherwise.

Real world

At Harrow Lane (constructed), the reliability engineer and the analyst argue for a while about whether 15 percent missing is "a lot". The planning lead stops them: the number is not the question. If the missing 15 percent were the quiet mid-life cycles, the planner would barely care. If it is the last weeks of each engine, it is all of the planner's decision. The size of a gap never tells you which.

Self-studyWhat the 1976 paper says, and what it does not3 min▶

Only the abstract of Rubin (1976) was read at source for tonight. It states two sets of conditions: for estimates built from sampling distributions, that the data are missing at random and observed at random; for likelihood or Bayesian estimates, that the data are missing at random and the parameter of the missing-data process is distinct from the one being estimated. It calls these the weakest general conditions under which ignoring the process is always appropriate.

The formal definitions, and the three-way naming used on this page (completely at random, at random, not at random), come from the full paper and from later texts; that is reported here, not read. The leader-level reading above does not depend on the fine print: if the gap depends on the missing value itself, you are outside the conditions, and the surviving rows are no longer a fair sample.

Imputation methods and missingness as a model feature are taught in learn feature engineering, practitioner session 7, missing data as signal. They are not retaught here.

Part 2 · covers dropout when conditions are worst

A sensor goes quiet when the machine is struggling

8 min live

In leader session 1 the exhaust temperature, T50, barely moved for seven tenths of each engine's life and then climbed. So "the probe quits when it runs hot" is not a rule about temperature; it is a rule about age. The figure draws one life from first cycle to failure, the line where a reading counts as hot, and the readings that never arrive.

LiveWorst conditions are the conditions that matter3 min▶

Across all 100 engines, 1.6 percent of the rows in the first half of each life are at or above 1416.2 degrees Rankine, the threshold that marks the hottest fifth of all readings. In each engine's last 30 cycles, 88.8 percent are. A device that struggles with heat therefore loses almost nothing while the engine is healthy and most of what it would have said in the cycles before failure.

The leader's point is general. Whatever makes a device quit (heat, vibration, a saturated link, a sagging supply) tends to peak when the machine is under stress, and a machine is most under stress near the end of its life. The rows a remaining-life model most needs are the rows most likely to be absent. That is why the title of the session is a working assumption, not a slogan: treat every gap as not at random until someone shows you the reason.

LiveHow Harrow Lane plants the dropout3 min▶

The NASA file has no gaps, so Harrow Lane (constructed) adds them on purpose, with a rule written down before anything is measured. Every reading of T50 at or above 1416.2 is removed with probability 0.7, using a seeded random draw so the same rows vanish every time. That removes 3,073 of the 20,631 rows, 14.9 percent. Nothing else is changed: the cooler readings all arrive, and the other thirteen channels are not touched except that a row missing its T50 is a row with a gap.

Planting it is the point. On a real feed the reason for each gap is unknown and has to be argued about. Here the reason is declared, so the bench can show exactly what the cheapest response does to the estimate. The dropout is modelled, labelled modelled; the readings it removes are real public rows from NASA's simulation, in which the degradation itself is simulated (C-MAPSS).

Real world

Harrow Lane's reliability engineer (constructed) is the first to notice something off: the feed's dashboard shows the fleet's exhaust readings getting steadier and cooler over the quarter, while the hangar is busier than ever. The readings are not wrong. Each one that arrives is accurate. The ones that would have shown the climb are the ones that never arrive.

Self-studyWhy a file with no nulls can hide the same problem2 min▶

Plenty of pipelines never show a null at all. A gateway that loses a reading may send the last value again, or a default, or simply nothing, so the row is never written and the table has no hole to count. Each of those makes the same kind of gap as tonight's, only harder to see: the completeness check reports 100 percent while the hot end of the story is missing or frozen.

The question to ask the people who maintain a device is therefore not "how many nulls" but "what does the device do when it struggles", which was the homework from leader session 1. The build-along below turns the answer into a line in a ledger.

Part 3 · covers what deletion does to the estimate

Drop the gaps and the engine looks healthier

8 min live

Now the pipeline does the tidy thing: it drops every row with a gap and hands the rest to the model, which estimates each engine's remaining life from its most recent readings. LiveWhy deletion flatters a failing engine3 min▶

The model estimates remaining life from the most recent readings it holds. After the drop, the most recent row it holds is often not today's: it is the last cycle that was cool enough to be reported. The model treats that older, cooler row as the engine's current state, so it sees more health and predicts more life than is left. Across the 300 units under test the mean error moves from 1.4 cycles late to 4.7 cycles late, and the share of estimates that land late rises from 56 to 60 percent.

Not every engine moves the same way: pick a few in the widget and some barely shift or even move earlier, because a seeded random draw decides which hot readings vanish and each engine's model error starts from a different place. The pattern across the fleet is what the planner lives with, and it leans late. For an engine-overhaul shop, late is the expensive direction: an engine left in service that fails there.

LiveTwo numbers, two different stories3 min▶

The bench reports the same 300 estimates two ways. Root mean square error moves from 23.64 to 24.89: up 1.25, about 5 percent. A data team glancing at that would call the dropout a rounding problem. The PHM08 asymmetric score, the one published with this data, moves from 3,366 to 4,656: up 1,290, about 38 percent. The score grows exponentially with the error and punishes late more steeply than early (it divides a late miss by 10 and an early miss by 13 before exponentiating), so a modest slide to the late side costs far more than square error admits.

So the same planted fault is "about 5 percent worse" or "about 38 percent worse" depending on which number the report leads with. A leader who only ever sees RMSE will never learn that the cleaning step pushed the fleet's estimates late. Leader session 3 takes the score apart; tonight the habit is to ask for both numbers, and for the bias.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree3 min▶

Should a bench report one score or several? The paper that published this data used one summed asymmetric score to rank the 2008 challenge entries, and in the same paper its authors argued for more than one number: weighting short true lives more heavily, and adding a correlation measure, because a single summed score can hide uneven performance across units. Tonight's two-number gap is a live example of why.

Others prefer one headline number precisely because several let a team pick the flattering one. Harrow Lane's bench prints RMSE, the summed score, the bias and the share predicted late side by side and crowns none of them. Which one your organisation leads with is a decision about what kind of mistake it most needs to see, and it should be made before the dropout, not after.

Self-studyWhat the model in the widget is2 min▶

It is the best rung of Harrow Lane's bench: the fourteen moving channels combined into one health index, a remaining-life target capped at 125 cycles (a convention chosen on this subset; 130 is also in use, and the bench prints both), and a short lag-free smoothing over the last ten health-index values. The smoothing's gain is small, 0.36 of RMSE at the canon settings, and a trailing mean over the same ten values lags and makes both numbers worse; it is not a fix for noise, and it is no fix for dropout either.

The model is fitted on 70 training engines and tested at 10 seeded cut points on each of the 30 held-out engines, so n = 300 units under test. The held-out engines stand in for the NASA test set, which is not used. Practitioner session 6 builds the planting and the measurement step by step.

Part 4 · build-along

A gap ledger for one feed you own

15 min hands on

No code. One feed, one page, three questions per gap: when does it go missing, what does the pipeline put in its place, and which way does that push the decision. The figure is the mechanism behind the second question: the same gap, filled three ways, and only one of them was measured tonight.

Pick one feed and one decision

The same feed you used for leader session 1's homework, or any device-written feed that drives a decision: pull a machine, dispatch a crew, raise an alarm. Write the decision in one sentence and name who makes it.

Ask when it goes missing

Not how often: when. Ask the people who maintain the device and the people who run the pipeline. Write the answer as "readings go missing when..." and mark whether that moment is near the conditions your decision is about. If it is, assume not at random until shown otherwise.

Ask what the pipeline puts in the gap

Drop the row, repeat the last value, or write a default: pick the one your pipeline actually does, from the people who wrote it, not from the dashboard. Many feeds do more than one at different stages. Write each down.

Ask which way it pushes the decision

For each fill, say in plain words whether it makes the machine look healthier, sicker or merely steadier than it is. On Harrow Lane's bench, dropping the rows pushed the fleet's estimates late. Mark the direction your team believes, and whether anyone has measured it.

Ask which number would show it

Find the one accuracy figure your team reports for this model. Would it move if the estimates slid late by a few cycles? Tonight RMSE moved about 5 percent while the asymmetric score moved about 38 percent. If the reported number is RMSE alone, ask for the bias beside it.

Write the one line for the planner

"This feed goes missing when ..., the pipeline fills the gap by ..., and that probably makes our estimates ... ." One line, in the planner's words. A blank in that line is the finding to take to leader session 3.

The habit to keep. Never accept a completeness percentage on its own. Ask when the gaps happen, what fills them, and which way that pushes the decision. A gap is never just smaller data; it is a claim about why the data is not there.

Check yourself

Three questions

Harrow Lane's analyst says the exhaust feed is 85 percent complete and proposes dropping the rows with gaps, because "15 percent is small". What should a leader ask first?

The size of a gap never says whether it may be ignored; the reason does. If the probe quits on hot cycles, the missing 15 percent is the end of each engine's life, and dropping it is a claim Rubin's conditions do not support.

On Harrow Lane's bench, after the planted dropout and deletion, RMSE moves from 23.64 to 24.89 and the asymmetric score from 3,366 to 4,656. What is the right reading?

The mean error went from +1.4 to +4.7 cycles. A late slide of a few cycles adds little to square error but a lot to an exponential score that divides a late miss by 10 and an early one by 13. Both numbers are honest; they answer different questions.

Why does dropping the rows with gaps make a failing engine look healthier?

The planted rule removes only hot readings, and hot readings live near the end of life. After the drop the model's "latest" view of the engine is a cooler moment from earlier, so it predicts more life than is left.
