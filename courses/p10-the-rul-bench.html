The remaining-useful-life bench

Nine sessions took a remaining-life estimate apart one piece at a time. This one puts the whole ladder in front of you on the 100 simulated engines: the fleet-average floor, a straight linear fit, a health index trained on a capped label, and lag-free smoothing on top, each scored on the same 300 held-out cut points with root mean square error and the PHM08 asymmetric score side by side. Then two buttons. One plants the most ordinary sensor fault there is, readings that vanish when the engine runs hot, and lets the pipeline drop the rows with gaps. The other shuffles the order of cycles inside every engine, which breaks the bench on purpose and proves it was measuring time. Real public rows from NASA's simulation, computed in your browser.

LiveWhat the bench recomputes on every press4 min▶

A seeded split sets 30 of the 100 engines aside. Each held-out engine is cut at 10 points, with true remaining life drawn from 10 to 150 cycles, giving 300 units under test. The other 70 engines train. Every rung standardises the 14 channels on the training rows, fits by least squares, predicts at each cut point using only the rows up to it, and is scored on all 300.

The rungs: the fleet-average life minus age; a linear fit of the uncapped label on the raw channels; the same fit on a label capped at 125 and divided by it, which makes the fitted value a health index between 0 and 1; and that health index smoothed by a straight line through its last 10 values, read at the newest point. Every number is computed in the page from real public rows of NASA's simulation. The split, the cut points, the dropout and the shuffle are modelled, and labelled so on the widget.

LiveThe five runs worth doing first6 min▶
  • Leave everything alone. Floor 45.61, linear 30.50, health index 24.00, smoothed 23.64; scores 305,167, 28,557, 3,542 and 3,366 summed over 300 units. Late share 43, 70, 56 and 56 percent.
  • Read the linear rung's bias. +15.8 cycles: without a cap, the fit is dragged by early-life rows it cannot distinguish, and it ends up late on 70 percent of units. Late is the expensive direction, so its score is 28,557 even though its error is "only" 30.50.
  • Switch the cap to 130. The smoothed rung reads 23.70 and 3,622 against 23.64 and 3,366. A convention, chosen on this subset; neither value is right.
  • Try seeds 1, 7 and 42. The numbers move. What holds on every seed: the capped health index beats the floor and the linear fit, the lag-free smoother beats the trailing mean, the dropout makes both numbers worse than the clean top rung (on the score, under the late-is-worse lean this bench uses; session 7 shows the swap), and the shuffle drags the top rung back near the floor. What does not hold: at seed 7 the uncapped linear fit is worse than the floor (40.40 against 38.06), and at seed 42 the 130 cap beats 125 (21.89 against 22.32). Quote seed-2008 numbers and say "your numbers move with the seed".
  • Press the dropout button. 24.89 and 4,656. Part 2 is about why.
Self-studyWhy the floor's score is 305,1674 min▶

A sum of exponentials is ruled by its worst few terms. Engine 69 (life 362, the longest) and engine 96 (life 336) are already past the 200-cycle training average at their cut points, so the floor predicts 0 to 14 cycles left when 140 to 150 remain. Those six units, out of 300, carry 79.5 percent of the sum; the single worst (an error of -142 cycles) costs 55,440 on its own. For contrast, the smoothed rung's three worst units carry 12.8 percent of its total.

The paper that defined the score saw this coming. Saxena and colleagues suggest weighting short true lives more and adding a correlation measure, because one aggregate can hide a model that predicts well for some units and badly for the rest. The bench prints four numbers per rung for that reason.

Self-studyWhy smoothing buys so little, and how it can cost4 min▶

The health index is already a weighted sum of 14 channels, and averaging across channels removes much of the cycle-to-cycle noise before any smoothing in time. What is left is a signal that only moves one way, slowly at first and then quickly. A trailing mean of the last 10 values reports where the engine was about five cycles ago, which is always healthier than where it is: the bias goes from +1.4 to +4.3 and the score from 3,366 to 4,171.

The lag-free smoother fits a straight line through the same 10 values and reads it at the newest point. It removes noise without stepping back in time. On seeds 1, 7 and 42 it improved both numbers each time, by 0.03 to 0.85 cycles of error: real, small, and never a fix.

Part 2 · the anti-lever and the break button

A heat-driven dropout, and the button that removes time

14 min hands on

The dropout is planted and declared: any reading with T50 at or above 1416.2 degrees Rankine (the hottest fifth of the fleet's readings) goes missing with probability 0.7, and the pipeline then does what most pipelines do with a gap, which is drop the row. 3,073 of the 20,631 rows vanish, nearly all from the end of engines' lives. The figure draws what that does to one prediction.

LiveReading the two-number move5 min▶

Press the dropout button at the canon settings. Root mean square error goes from 23.64 to 24.89, five percent. The asymmetric score goes from 3,366 to 4,656, thirty-eight percent. The bias goes from +1.4 to +4.7 cycles and the late share from 56 to 60 percent. One number says "slightly worse". The other says "materially worse, and in the expensive direction".

That gap is the whole argument for printing both. A team that tracks only RMSE ships the dropout-damaged model and calls it a five percent regression. A team that tracks the score sees the late shift. Neither number alone tells you that the cause is the pipeline's treatment of missing rows; the mechanism in the figure does, and practitioner session 6 is where it was built.

Real world

Harrow Lane's analyst had a clean pipeline with a single line in it that nobody remembered writing: drop rows with any null. On the simulated fleet that line does exactly what the button does. Her fix was not a cleverer model; it was carrying the time gap forward, so the model knows how stale its last reading is, and refusing to call a prediction "current" when the newest reading was more than a few cycles old.

LiveWhy the break button proves the bench measures time4 min▶

Shuffle the order of the cycles inside every engine and keep the labels where they were. The channels still hold the same values, the fleet is the same, the fit is the same code. The only thing destroyed is the link between a reading and how far the engine is from failure. The top rung goes to 40.41 and 46,145, close to the floor's error, because every prediction collapses toward the fleet's middle.

A bench that scored about the same with time shuffled would be measuring something other than degradation. This one does not, and that is the evidence that its other numbers mean what they say.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree4 min▶

Which way does the score lean? The prose of the 2008 paper says late predictions are penalised more, and its Figure 9 shows a score of about 147 at an error of +50 against about 46 at -50. Its equation as printed pairs the parameter 10 with early errors and 13 with late ones, which taken literally would do the opposite. The bench follows the prose and the figure (late divides by 10, early by 13). Expect to meet code that followed the equation.

One score or several? The same paper argues the summed score should be weighted toward short true lives and paired with a correlation measure. Later work mostly reports RMSE and the summed score. The bench prints four numbers and does not crown one.

125 or 130? Both caps are in current use. At seed 2008 the difference is 23.64 against 23.70 in error and 3,366 against 3,622 in score; at seed 42 the order flips (22.32 against 21.89). A choice that flips with the split is a convention, not a finding.

Part 3 · build-along

Reproduce the canon, then write the brief

11 min hands on

Open the console. Reproduce the top rung, the anti-lever and the break, prove the self-checks can fail, then write five lines anyone signing off a remaining-life model should be handed. The figure is the brief.

Run the canon ladder

Every rung at the default settings. The top rung should print 23.64 and 3366; your numbers will match for the same seed.

consolevar r = IOT.ladder(IOT_SAMPLE); [r.smooth.rmse.toFixed(2), r.smooth.score.toFixed(0), r.smooth.bias.toFixed(1), r.smooth.n]

Read the anti-lever and the break

Both were computed in the same call. 24.89 and 4656 for the dropout, with 3,073 rows removed; 40.41 and 46145 for the shuffle.

console[r.mnarSmooth.rmse.toFixed(2), r.mnarSmooth.score.toFixed(0), r.mnar.dropped, r.shuffleSmooth.rmse.toFixed(2), r.shuffleSmooth.score.toFixed(0)]

Prove a self-check can fail

Plant the swapped score back into the real function: the check "late costs more than early at equal size" must fail. Then the lagging smoother and the dropout that removes cool rows too. A check that passed on the broken engine would not be a check.

consoleIOT.FAULTS.map(function (f) { var t = IOT.selfTest(f); return f + ": " + (t.pass ? "PASSED (bad)" : "failed " + t.checks.filter(function (c) { return !c.pass; }).map(function (c) { return c.name; }).join("; ")); })

Move the seed and watch the order hold

Seed 42 gives different numbers: 22.32 for the clean top rung, 23.16 with the dropout, 39.28 shuffled. Check that the dropout is still worse than the clean rung on both scores and the shuffle far worse than both. The numbers move with the seed; that ordering held on every seed tried.

consolevar s = IOT.ladder(IOT_SAMPLE, { seed: 42, only: ["smooth", "mnarSmooth", "shuffleSmooth"] }); [s.smooth.rmse, s.mnarSmooth.rmse, s.shuffleSmooth.rmse].map(function (x) { return x.toFixed(2); })

Write the brief for Harrow Lane

Five lines as in the figure, filled from your own runs. For line 3, write the mechanism of missingness in one sentence ("readings vanish when..."), then what the pipeline does with a gap. If the answer is "drops the row", line 3 is the finding.

The habit to keep. Never sign off a remaining-life number without its floor, its cap, its missingness mechanism, both scores and its protocol. And never trust a bench you have not broken: if shuffling time does not wreck it, it was not measuring time.

Check yourself

Three questions

The dropout button moves the top rung's RMSE from 23.64 to 24.89 and its asymmetric score from 3,366 to 4,656. Why does the score move so much more?

Both numbers are on the same 300 units. What changed is the direction of the errors: removing the hot end of life makes engines look healthier, so remaining life is over-predicted. Late errors divide by 10 in the exponent, early by 13.

A colleague swaps the lag-free smoother for a trailing mean of the same 10 values "because it is simpler". What does the bench say?

Same window, different estimator. On a signal that only moves one way, lag is bias, and here the bias is in the expensive direction (+4.3 against +1.4).

What does the break button (shuffle cycle order inside each engine) prove?

Everything except the time order is held fixed. A bench that scored similarly with time destroyed would be measuring something else. This one collapses, which is what gives its other numbers their meaning.

After the track

Homework

About an hour, partly in the console here and partly on something you own.

Build the fix the anti-lever implies

Instead of dropping gappy rows, carry each row's cycle number into the smoother so the line is fitted against real time, not row position. Run it under the planted dropout and write down whether the late shift shrinks. Your numbers will differ; the direction is the result.

Break your own bench

Take a model you own that predicts something over time. Shuffle the time order of its inputs inside each entity and rescore. If the score barely moves, write down what it was actually measuring.

Write the five-line brief for a real model

Floor, label, missingness, both numbers, protocol. Hand it to whoever signs off the model and ask which line they would have asked for first.
