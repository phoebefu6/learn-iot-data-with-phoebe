From many channels to one health index

Session 3 sorted the channels. Fourteen survived, and nobody can read fourteen traces per engine per week. Part 0

Harrow Lane's planners want one gauge

Harrow Lane Overhaul is a constructed engine-overhaul shop whose fleet is NASA's 100 simulated engines. Every week its planners decide which engines to pull. The analyst has triaged the channels (session 3) and could hand them fourteen charts per engine. They asked for something else: one gauge per engine, full when it is healthy, empty when it is about to fail, that they can sort a list by. Building that gauge is easy. Knowing what it hides is the work.

★ What you walk out with tonight. The three moves that turn many channels into one number (standardise on the training engines only, weight by least squares, combine and clamp) built by hand in your console and checked against the engine to three decimals. The paper's own health index, the lowest of four normalised operability margins, and why participants were never given it. A learned index scored on 300 held-out cut points: root mean square error 24.00 cycles and an asymmetric score of 3,542, against 30.50 and 28,557 for the same fourteen channels fitted to the raw label. And an honest account of where that gain comes from, which is not where most people first look.

LiveWhere tonight sits2 min▶

The data is NASA's FD001 training set, data set 6 of the Prognostics Center of Excellence repository (Saxena and Goebel, 2008): 100 engines, one operating condition, one fault mode, each run from a healthy start to failure. The degradation is simulated with C-MAPSS, so every number on this page describes real public rows from NASA's simulation, not real engines.

Session 1 found the label in the cycle count. Session 3 decided which fourteen channels a model may see. Tonight is the first model that uses them as one signal, and it is rung 2 of the bench that session 10 runs end to end.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back2 min▶
  • Health index. One number per engine per cycle, 1 when healthy and 0 at failure.
  • Standardise. Subtract a channel's mean and divide by its standard deviation, so every channel is measured in the same unit: standard deviations.
  • Weight. How much one standard deviation of a channel moves the index. Fitted, not chosen.
  • Operability margin. How far a component is from a limit it must not cross, such as a compressor's stall margin.
  • Capped label. Remaining life held flat at 125 early in life, so the index reads 1 until the engine has fewer than 125 cycles left.
  • Cut point. A moment in a held-out engine's life where the model is asked "how long is left?". 30 engines, 10 cut points each, 300 units under test.
Part 1 · covers standardise, weight, combine

Fourteen rulers, one number

12 min live

The fourteen channels are measured in degrees Rankine, psia, rpm and plain ratios. Adding them as they stand would add units, not evidence. The figure draws the three moves that fix that, with the numbers the engine prints for the seed-2008 training engines.

LiveStandardise: one ruler for every channel4 min▶

Each channel gets its own mean and standard deviation, computed on the 14,026 rows of the 70 training engines and on nothing else. T50 (the low-pressure turbine outlet temperature) centres on 1408.91 degrees Rankine with a spread of 8.97; the core speed Nc on 9066.51 rpm with a spread of 22.78; the bypass ratio on 8.44 with a spread of 0.037. After subtracting and dividing, a reading of +1 means "one spread above the training average" on every channel alike.

Two things this move does not do. It does not remove noise: a jittery channel is exactly as jittery in standard deviations as it was in rpm (session 3's noise share is unchanged by it). And it must not see the held-out engines. A mean computed on all 100 engines would leak a little of the test engines' end of life into the ruler, and the score would flatter the model by an amount nobody could see.

Real world

Harrow Lane's analyst (constructed, like the shop) first tried the planners' own suggestion: add up the fourteen readings and watch the total. Its movement was mostly core speed and corrected core speed, the two channels whose spread is counted in tens of rpm, and T50's rise of about 22 degrees over a life, the signal her engineers trusted most, barely showed in it. She standardised, and the same sum stopped being a speed chart in disguise.

LiveWeight: the sign from triage, the size from the fit4 min▶

The weights are fitted by least squares on the training rows, to a target that reads 1 while an engine has 125 or more cycles left and falls in a straight line to 0 at failure. All fourteen signs agree with session 3's correlations: the channels that rise as failure nears (T50, Ps30, Nc and the rest) get weights below zero, and the four that fall (P30, phi, W31, W32) get weights above zero. That agreement is a check worth running every time; a sign that disagrees with the triage is a question to answer before anyone reads the index.

The sizes are where people overread. The largest weight belongs to Nc, -0.052 per standard deviation, the channel with one of the weakest correlations in session 3 (-0.390). That is not a contradiction. Nc drifts steadily across the whole life, which a one-line correlation flattens and a joint fit can use. And Nc and NRc are one shaft read two ways (session 1), as are Nf and NRf; how a fit splits weight between two readings of the same thing says nothing about either probe. A weight is a coefficient in one fitted sum, not an importance score.

Self-studyCombine: a sum, a floor and a ceiling2 min▶

The index is 0.686 plus the sum of weight times standardised reading, then held between 0 and 1. The 0.686 is what an engine reads when every channel sits exactly at the training average, and it is also the average of the capped target over the training rows: most of those rows come from the part of life with 125 or more cycles left, so the average engine reads well above one half. The floor and ceiling matter more than they look: without them a very healthy reading would promise more than 125 cycles, which the target never taught, and a very hot one would promise negative life.

Multiply by 125 and the index becomes cycles left, which is how the bench scores it. Equal weights on the sign-corrected channels are the obvious alternative and a fair homework exercise; the bench does not run them, so no number for them appears here.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree4 min▶

Where the index should top out. The learned index reads 1 at 125 cycles left because the target is capped at 125. Papers use 125 and 130 both: one sets 125 "based on the minimum run length of 128 cycles observed in the training data", another uses a maximum of 130, and the paper usually credited with the capped target, Heimes (2008), is reported second-hand only. Move the cap and the index's whole scale moves with it. It is a convention, chosen on this subset; it is a modelling choice, not a law of engines. Session 5 runs both.

Whether to fuse at all. The PHM08 paper argues that one summed score can hide uneven performance across units and asks for more than one number when judging models. The same objection applies to a fused index: one number per engine is what the planners asked for, and it is also exactly where the channel that moved disappears. Some teams keep the index and a short list of the channels driving it side by side; some keep only the channels. Neither side has a result on this bench that settles it.

Part 2 · covers the two kinds of index

The index the paper kept back, and the one you can learn

12 min live

The simulation that produced these rows had a health index of its own, built from physics, and it decided when each engine failed. Nobody outside got it. The learned index is an attempt to infer it from the sensors, and the figure puts the two side by side so the difference in kind is impossible to miss.

LiveThe index the paper defined and kept back4 min▶

Saxena, Goebel, Simon and Eklund (2008) describe how the runs were made. Each engine's health is the lowest of its normalised operability margins: the stall margins of the fan, the low-pressure compressor and the high-pressure compressor, and the exhaust gas temperature (EGT) margin. It reads 1 when healthy and 0 at failure; the stall margins reach their limit at 15 percent and the EGT margin at about 2 percent, and the simulation stopped when health reached 0. Participants in the PHM08 challenge were not given this index. They had to infer it from the sensed variables, which is the job every practitioner session inherits.

Notice the operator. A lowest-of-four index is ruled by whichever margin is closest to its limit; the other three can be anywhere. In FD001 there is one fault mode, high-pressure compressor degradation, so one margin is expected to lead. In a subset with two fault modes, the margin that rules the index can differ from engine to engine, and one number would not say which.

LiveReading the widget, and where the gain comes from4 min▶

Start on rung 2 and engine 96, one of the two long-lived engines (life 336). Its gauge column empties as the cut points move toward failure, and its two cut points with the most life left (143 and 150 cycles) both read well under 1. Then read the bench line: on 300 cut points across 30 held-out engines (not the NASA test set), rung 2 scores a root mean square error of 24.00 cycles and a summed asymmetric score of 3,542, with a mean bias of +0.8 and 56 percent of units predicted late. Switch to rung 1: 30.50 and 28,557, and 70 percent late. Then look back at engine 96 alone under each rung: its own error does not follow the fleet's ranking, which is the uneven-units warning in miniature.

Now the honest part. Rung 2 differs from rung 1 in what it is fitted to, not in which channels it sees. Dividing the target by 125 divides every fitted weight by 125 and changes no prediction at all; multiply the index back by 125 and you have a straight linear fit to the capped label, held between 0 and 125. So the drop from 30.50 to 24.00 is not something "having an index" earned. It comes from the capped target and the clamp. The widget prints one visible piece of it: every held-out cut point whose true life is above 125 is predicted early, because the index cannot say more than 125, and early is the cheaper side of a score that divides late errors by 10 and early ones by 13. Session 5 takes the target apart.

Self-studyWhat one number hides4 min▶
  • Which channel moved. Two engines at 0.4 can get there by different routes. The sum is the same; the maintenance conversation is not.
  • Anything above the cap. An engine with 150 cycles left and one with 130 both read healthy. For a planner choosing between them, that is information thrown away by design.
  • Noise. The index is a weighted sum of noisy readings, and nothing in the three moves smooths it. Session 10's rung 3 adds a lag-free local line over the last 10 values; the gain on the bench is small (0.36 RMSE), and a plain trailing mean of the same window makes both numbers worse, because a signal that only moves one way punishes lag.
  • Uneven engines. One fleet RMSE averages over engines the index reads well and engines it reads badly. The widget prints each held-out engine's own error; read the spread before quoting the average.
Real world

Harrow Lane's planners (constructed) sorted their first weekly list by the index and pulled the lowest three. The analyst added one column beside it: the two channels contributing most to each engine's drop that week. On the second week, two engines at nearly the same index had different leading channels, and the engineers wanted to see one of them first. The index sorted the list; the extra column decided the order of work.

Part 3 · build-along

Build the index by hand, then check it against the bench

17 min hands on

Open the browser console on this page (the sample and the engine are already loaded). You will build Harrow Lane's gauge for one engine by hand, check it against the engine to three decimals, and reproduce rung 2 of the bench. The figure shows which engines feed which step, because the line between them is where most index projects leak.

Decode the fleet and split it

The same seeded split every practitioner session uses: 70 training engines, 30 held out, 10 cut points each. You should see 70, 30 and 300.

consolevar units = IOT.decode(IOT_SAMPLE); var sp = IOT.splitAndCuts(units, IOT.DEFAULTS); [sp.trainIds.length, sp.testIds.length, sp.cuts.length]

Build the ruler on the training engines only

A mean and a standard deviation per channel, from training rows and nothing else. You should see 14,026 rows, and T50 (the third channel) centred on 1408.91 with a spread of 8.97.

consolevar rows = [].concat.apply([], sp.trainIds.map(function (id) { return units[id - 1].rows; })); var mu = [], sd = []; for (var k = 0; k < 14; k++) { var m = rows.reduce(function (a, x) { return a + x[k]; }, 0) / rows.length; var v = rows.reduce(function (a, x) { return a + (x[k] - m) * (x[k] - m); }, 0) / rows.length; mu.push(m); sd.push(Math.sqrt(v)); } [rows.length, mu[2].toFixed(2), sd[2].toFixed(2)]

Fit the weights

Rung 2 of the bench fits an intercept and 14 weights to the capped label over 125; rung 1 fits the same channels to the raw label, for comparison. You should see 15 numbers, the first 0.686.

consolevar r = IOT.ladder(IOT_SAMPLE, { only: ["linear", "hi"] }); [r.hi.w.length, r.hi.w[0].toFixed(3)]

Check every sign against the triage

Session 3's correlation and tonight's weight should point the same way on every channel. You should see 14, then the weights by channel: Nc's -0.052 is the largest in size.

consolevar tri = IOT.triage(IOT_SAMPLE).kept; tri.filter(function (t, k) { return (t.r > 0) === (r.hi.w[k + 1] > 0); }).length; tri.map(function (t, k) { return t.sym + " " + r.hi.w[k + 1].toFixed(3); }).join(", ")

Compute one engine's index by hand

The first cut point is engine 96 at cycle 262, with 74 cycles truly left. Intercept plus weight times standardised reading gives 0.540, the same as the engine's own prediction divided by 125; that is 67.5 cycles, early by 6.5.

consolevar c = r.cuts[0], row = units[c.u - 1].rows[c.cut - 1]; var hi = r.hi.w[0]; for (var k = 0; k < 14; k++) hi += r.hi.w[k + 1] * (row[k] - mu[k]) / sd[k]; [c.u, c.cut, c.rul, hi.toFixed(3), (r.hi.preds[0] / 125).toFixed(3), r.hi.preds[0].toFixed(1)]

Score it, and count the cut points above the cap

On the 300 held-out cut points (not the NASA test set): rung 2 at 24.00 and 3,542, rung 1 at 30.50 and 28,557. Then 49 cut points have more than 125 cycles truly left, and the index predicts all 49 early. Your numbers match for the same seed.

console[r.hi.rmse.toFixed(2), Math.round(r.hi.score), r.linear.rmse.toFixed(2), Math.round(r.linear.score)]; var above = r.cuts.filter(function (c) { return c.rul > 125; }).length; var early = r.cuts.filter(function (c, i) { return c.rul > 125 && r.hi.preds[i] < c.rul; }).length; [above, early]

The habit to keep. Before anyone reads an index, write three lines beside it: which rows built the ruler (training only), whether every weight's sign agrees with the triage, and the highest value the index can say. The third line is the one people forget, and it decides which side of the truth the index lands on for every healthy engine.

Check yourself

Three questions

Why are the channels standardised, with mean and spread from the training engines only, before they are weighted?

Noise in standard deviations is the same noise, and least squares fits raw values happily, with weights that mostly measure units. The training-only ruler is what keeps the held-out score honest.

On 300 held-out cut points, rung 2 (health index, label capped at 125) scores 24.00 and 3,542; rung 1 (same channels, raw label) scores 30.50 and 28,557. What earned the gain?

Both rungs see the same fourteen channels, and nothing in the three moves smooths noise. What changed is the target and the ceiling; one visible effect is that all 49 cut points above 125 are now predicted early, the cheaper side of the score.

What was the health index in the PHM08 simulation, and who had it?

The paper's index is physical and was kept back so that participants had to infer it. A weighted sum of sensors fitted to a capped label is one way of inferring it, not the thing itself.

Homework

Thirty minutes, in the console on this page. Your numbers will differ from anything printed here as soon as you change an input, and that is the point.

Drop one reading of a shaft and refit

Using the standardised training rows from the build-along and IOT.fitOLS, refit the capped label without NRc (the corrected core speed) and compare Nc's new weight with -0.052. Write one sentence on what the change says about reading a weight as an importance.

Find the engine the index reads worst

Step through the held-out engines in the widget and note the one with the largest error over its 10 cut points. Look at its rows in session 1's inspector: which channel looks different from the fleet, and would one index have told a planner?

Write the three lines for a score you own

Any composite you have shipped (a risk score, a health score, a lead score): which rows built its ruler, whether its weights' signs match a simple one-variable check, and the highest value it can say. Session 5 asks the same of the label.

Take this with you

Cheat sheet

Three moves. Standardise, weight, combine. Only the weighting learns anything about failure.
The ruler. Mean and spread from training engines only. Never let held-out rows shape it.
The sign check. Every weight's sign should match the triage correlation. All 14 do here.
Weights are not importances. Nc carries the largest weight with one of the weakest correlations; twin readings split weight arbitrarily.
The paper's index. Lowest normalised operability margin, 1 to 0. Kept back from participants.
The learned index. A fitted proxy for the capped label, held between 0 and 1. Times 125 for cycles.
The bench. Rung 2: RMSE 24.00, score 3,542 on 300 held-out cut points, not the NASA test set.
The ceiling. An index capped at 125 (or 130) is early on every engine healthier than that.
