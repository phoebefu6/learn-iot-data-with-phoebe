Why the warehouse playbook breaks here

A data team that has shipped dashboards, churn models and demand forecasts arrives on a sensor floor with a playbook that works. Five of its habits quietly fail here, and each fails for a reason you can draw: the row has no author, the unit has no user, the truth arrives once, acting on the answer removes the question, and a miss is priced by which side it lands on. Harrow Lane Overhaul, a constructed engine-overhaul shop, has a proposal on the table written from that playbook. Tonight is the five mechanisms, then a review of the proposal against everything sessions 1 to 4 taught. The rows are real public rows from NASA's simulation, not real engines, and nothing here is purchasing advice.

Part 0

Harrow Lane, the week the warehouse team offers to help

Harrow Lane Overhaul is a constructed shop whose planners decide, each week, which engines to pull for maintenance. Its fleet, as in every session, is the 100 turbofan engines of NASA's FD001 training set: real public rows from NASA's simulation, the degradation simulated with C-MAPSS, every engine run cycle by cycle until it failed. A data team from the group's retail side (constructed too, and unnamed) has offered to "do predictive maintenance" with the playbook that served it well on orders and customers. The proposal is short, confident and built entirely from good habits. Each habit meets a mechanism on the sensor floor that it was never designed for.

LiveWhy a good playbook misfires here3 min▶

The warehouse playbook is not wrong; it is tuned to a world with five quiet properties. A row is written by a person or a system that meant it. Every row joins to someone who can be asked. The outcome you predict is a column you can look up. Acting on a prediction changes behaviour but still lets you see what happened. And a mistake costs about the same in either direction, so an average error is a fair summary.

A sensor floor removes all five, one at a time. None of the removals shows up as a failed data-quality check, which is why a capable team walks straight past them. The rest of the session takes them in order and draws the mechanism behind each, because a slogan ("IoT is different") changes nobody's plan, and a drawing of where the outcome goes missing does.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • Source. What wrote the row. In a warehouse, a person or a system acting on purpose; here, a device that reports whatever it reads.
  • Device baseline. The minimum capabilities a connected device should have before anyone trusts it. NIST IR 8259A names six.
  • Unit. The thing a row is about. Here an engine and its cycle count, or a household; never a user.
  • Ground truth. The outcome you check a prediction against. For a run-to-failure engine it is one event, at the end.
  • Censored. Known only as "not yet". Every cycle before failure says the engine has not failed, and nothing about when it will.
  • Feedback loop. Acting on a prediction changes the data the next model learns from. On a maintenance floor it removes the very outcome.
  • The lean. Which side of a miss the score punishes harder. Harrow Lane's bench uses late is worse: divide by 10 when late, by 13 when early.
  • Constructed. Harrow Lane, Flat 4B, the retail data team and its proposal are invented so that the truth is knowable. No figure describes a real engine, plant, household or team.
Part 1 · covers the source, the identity and the ground truth

Who wrote the row, who it is about, and when you find out

15 min live

Three contrasts sit side by side in the figure. Each column puts the warehouse habit on top and the sensor-floor mechanism underneath. Read down a column, not across: the top box is what the playbook assumes without saying so, and the copper mark below is the thing that assumption cannot see.

LiveThe source: a device that drifts and never says so5 min▶

In a warehouse a row has an author. A clerk typed the order, a system raised the invoice because a rule told it to. When the row is wrong, it is usually wrong in a way a check can catch: a key that does not join, a total that does not reconcile, a date in the future. And there is someone to ask.

A probe on a duct has no intent. It writes what it reads, once a cycle, and when it drifts it writes a perfectly formatted number that is slightly off, forever. Leader session 1 showed the two sides of that in the same file: the exhaust temperature that climbs as the compressor degrades is the signal, and a probe ageing would produce the same shape. No column in the row says which.

What a leader can ask for is a device baseline. NIST IR 8259A sets out six capabilities a connected device should have: Device Identification, Device Configuration, Data Protection, Logical Access to Interfaces, Software Update, and Cybersecurity State Awareness. Its authors call the core baseline "a default for minimally securable devices", and say it "does not specify how" each capability is achieved. Notice what the six are about: knowing which device wrote the row, what it was set to, who could reach it and whether it was changed. None of them promises the reading is right. The baseline makes the source accountable; drift stays the data team's problem.

Real world

Harrow Lane's retail data team (constructed) asked its usual first question: "who owns this data?" In the warehouse that question has a name for an answer. Here the honest answer was a list: the probe, the unit it is fitted to, whoever last configured it, and the software it is running. The reliability engineer could answer the first two from the file. The last two were not recorded anywhere, and that blank is what the team wrote down first.

Drift in a pipeline (a feed that changes shape, volume or freshness) is a different object, taught in learn data observability, distribution drift. Here the drift is in the physical sensor, and no pipeline monitor can see it.

LiveThe identity: a unit, not a user4 min▶

A warehouse row joins to a customer id, and a customer id joins to a person. That join carries two things nobody writes down: someone can confirm the row, and rows from different customers are roughly interchangeable, so splitting them at random into training and test is usually harmless.

On the sensor floor the unit is an engine and a cycle count. Engine 69 is 362 rows of one machine, each cycle sharing that engine's own starting wear; the dataset's readme says every engine "starts with different degrees of initial wear and manufacturing variation which is unknown to the user". Split those rows at random and some of engine 69's cycles land in training while its neighbours land in test. The model is then marked on an engine it has already seen, and the score flatters it. Harrow Lane's bench avoids that by holding out whole engines: a seeded split puts 70 engines in training and 30 in the held-out set, and no engine is on both sides.

At home the unit is a household. Leader session 4 showed Flat 4B's meter (constructed) writing one reading every 15 minutes for everyone in the flat at once. There is no user id to join to, and "the user" is the wrong word in any spec built on that trace.

LiveThe ground truth: one failure, at the end4 min▶

A churn label, a return flag, a delivered status: in a warehouse the outcome is a column, there any day you query it, and a second query can check it. You can label last month's customers this morning.

An engine in FD001 fails exactly once, at its last cycle. Every row before that is censored: it says "not yet" and nothing about when. The quantity that would tell you how close each engine was, the health index, exists inside the simulation (the lowest of four normalised operating margins, 1 healthy and 0 failed) and was deliberately not given to anyone using the data; it has to be inferred from the readings. And in the 2008 challenge the truth moved under the participants' feet: Saxena and colleagues report that the test set's true remaining lives ran from 10 to 150 cycles, while the validation set's ran from 6 to 190, a widening that was not announced.

For a leader the consequence is plain. You cannot label the fleet this morning, the label you get arrives once per machine, and the range of truth your model will face is itself something you may not be told.

LiveThe feedback loop: acting removes the outcome3 min▶

Warehouse models have feedback loops too. A recommendation changes what people buy; a discount changes demand. But the outcome is still logged: the sale happens or it does not, and next quarter's data records it.

On a maintenance floor the loop is harsher. The day Harrow Lane's planner starts acting on a model, every engine the model flags is pulled before it fails, and its failure is never observed. Would it have failed next week, or run safely to the check after next? The log cannot say. The engines that do fail in service are the ones the model missed. Retrain on that log and the model learns from a dataset its own decisions shaped, with the clearest failures removed.

FD001 has none of this, and that is exactly why it exists: every engine ran to failure because it is a simulation. A shop's own records never will, because the point of maintenance is that machines do not run to failure. Treat any training set drawn from a maintained fleet as censored by the maintenance, and ask what the log writes when an engine is pulled early.

LiveThe cost of being wrong: priced by its side3 min▶

A warehouse forecast that is too high by some amount costs roughly what one too low by the same amount costs, so an average error is a fair summary. A remaining-life estimate does not work that way. Too early pulls an engine with life left in it: hangar time, a spare, a line that waits. Too late leaves an engine in service past the point it fails. The costs are physical, and they are not equal.

The 2008 benchmark prices that directly. Its score grows exponentially with the error and leans on lateness: late misses are divided by 10 and early ones by 13 before the exponential, so a miss of 50 cycles costs about 46 when early and about 147 when late. That is the lean Harrow Lane's bench uses throughout, and leader session 3 set out how to choose it as a business decision.

The break button is the proof that time order carries the signal. Shuffle the cycles inside every engine, leave the labels where they were, and fit the same top rung. RMSE goes from 23.64 to 40.41 and, under the late-is-worse lean, the summed score from 3,366 to 46,145, over 300 cut points on 30 held-out engines. A warehouse table can usually be reordered without changing what it means. This one cannot: order is the data.

Real world

The retail team's first draft (constructed) built its training table the way it always had: one row per cycle, every column a feature, rows shuffled before a random split. Every cell value was correct. The reliability engineer asked them to run the widget above before arguing the point. Nobody argued.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree3 min▶

Which way does the score lean? The paper that defines it says in prose that late predictions are punished more heavily, and its plotted curve agrees: about 46 at 50 cycles early, about 147 at 50 cycles late. Its printed equation pairs the two constants the other way round, which, read literally, would punish early harder. The figure matches the stated intent, and the bench follows the figure and the prose. Later papers you meet may have picked either reading, so ask which one a number was scored under.

The lean matters for more than the size of a number. With the constants swapped, the score can rank a model fed by a heat-driven dropout above the clean one, as practitioner session 7 shows; RMSE gets worse under both readings. That is why every score on these pages names its lean.

One number or several? The same paper argues for weighting short remaining lives more and for a correlation measure beside the score, because a summed score can hide uneven units. The bench prints RMSE, score, bias and late share and does not crown one. Neither does this page.

Self-studyWhat the break proves, and what it does not2 min▶

Everything except time order is held fixed: the same 100 engines, the same channel values, the same split, the same code. A bench that scored about the same with time destroyed would be measuring something other than degradation. This one collapses, which is the evidence that its other numbers mean what they say.

It does not prove the model is good enough for any decision. It proves that an evaluation which ignores time order, a random row split or a shuffled feature table, is testing something else. The full ladder, with the break beside the anti-lever and the cap toggle, is in practitioner session 10.

Part 3 · build-along

Review a predictive-maintenance proposal in seven lines

17 min hands on

No code. The retail team's proposal (constructed) is six lines long, each one ordinary good practice in a warehouse. Read it against a checklist built from sessions 1 to 4 and tonight. The seventh line is the one the proposal does not contain, and it is the one that matters most. The figure is the review sheet to keep.

Read the proposal once, as written

Six lines: use all 21 channels; drop rows with gaps; report one accuracy figure; score risk per operator account; split rows at random; retrain each quarter on the shop's own log. Before checking anything, write one sentence on what decision the model is meant to change and who makes it. If the proposal does not say, that is the first finding.

Check the channels and the gaps (sessions 1 and 2)

Seven of the 21 channels never move under FD001's single operating condition; ask that they stay out here, with the reason written down. Then ask when the feed goes missing. If the answer is "when it runs hot", dropping rows with gaps removes the end of every engine's life and makes it look healthier than it is. Write the line "the readings go missing when..." and refuse the proposal until it is filled in.

Check the number and the unit (sessions 3 and 4)

"One accuracy figure" hides which side the misses land on. Ask the team to set the lean first, signed by whoever owns the decision, and to report bias, late share and the worst few engines beside any summed score. Then replace "operator account" with the unit the rows are about: the engine. If the same proposal is reused for a home product, the unit is the household, and nobody in it is "the user".

Check the split (tonight, identity)

A random row split puts cycles of one engine on both sides, and the test then marks the model on engines it has seen. Ask for a split by whole engine, the way the bench holds out 30 of 100. Write the rule as one line the team can be held to: "no unit appears in both training and test".

Check the log it will learn from (tonight, the loop)

"Retrain on the shop's own log" sounds like good hygiene. Once the planner acts on the model, the engines it flags are pulled and their failures never happen. Ask what the log records for an engine pulled early, and how the team will tell "would have failed" from "was fine". If nobody knows, the retraining plan teaches the model from its own blind spot.

Ask for the line that is not there, and write the review

The proposal says nothing about time order. Ask for the break test before anything else: shuffle cycle order inside each unit and rescore. On Harrow Lane's bench the top rung goes from 23.64 and 3,366 to 40.41 and 46,145 (the score under the late-is-worse lean). A model whose numbers barely move under that shuffle is not measuring degradation. Then write the review as seven lines, one per check, each marked pass, fix, or blank.

The habit to keep. When a proposal arrives in warehouse language, translate each line back into its mechanism before judging it: who wrote the row, what the unit is, when the truth arrives, what acting will hide, and which miss costs more. Then ask for the one test the warehouse never needed: shuffle time and show me the numbers.

Check yourself

Three questions

Harrow Lane's retail data team proposes splitting the 20,631 rows of the fleet at random into training and test. What is the right objection?

The unit is the engine, not the row. Each engine carries its own unknown initial wear, so its cycles are not interchangeable with another engine's. A large test set full of seen engines is still a flattering test.

The planner starts pulling every engine the model flags. A year later the team retrains on the shop's own maintenance log. What has happened to the data?

Acting on a prediction removes the outcome it predicted. FD001 runs every engine to failure only because it is a simulation; a maintained fleet's log is censored by the maintenance itself.

Shuffling cycle order inside each engine takes the bench's top rung from RMSE 23.64 to 40.41, and its score from 3,366 to 46,145 under the late-is-worse lean. What should a leader take from it?

The shuffle is a deliberate break that proves the bench's numbers depend on degradation over time. The score moves more because it is exponential and leans on lateness; that is a property of the lean, not evidence that either number is better.

Before session 6

Homework

Thirty minutes, on a proposal you have seen and on the log your own operation keeps.

Run the seven-line review on a real proposal

The last sensor or maintenance project you approved, or the next one on your desk. One line per check: channels, gaps, the number, the unit, the split, the log, time order. Mark each pass, fix or blank, and count the blanks.

Find out what your log writes when a machine is serviced early

Ask the people who keep the maintenance records. Is an early pull recorded as a pull, a failure, or nothing? Could anyone later tell an engine that would have failed from one that was fine? The answer decides whether your data can ever train a model honestly.

Hold one device against the baseline

Pick one connected device you depend on and ask which of NIST IR 8259A's six capabilities it has: identification, configuration, data protection, logical access to interfaces, software update, cybersecurity state awareness. Bring the list to session 6, where the transfer map sorts what you already know from what you must learn.
