Learn IoT & Industrial Data with Phoebe · Leader session 4 of 6

The home is a sensor network

Three sessions on engines taught that a sensor row has no author, no intent and no user id. Smart home is the consumer face of the same problem, and it moves the stakes into the living room. The unit is a household, not a person; nobody types a row; and what the readings say about the people inside is inferred, not asked. Flat 4B, a constructed household with an electricity meter that reports every 15 minutes, shows how much one simple rule prints about its routine. Tonight is where the inference goes, who it is about, and which inferences a product team should refuse to make. Nothing here is legal advice; the law lives in the GDPR and PDPA sessions linked below.

Part 0

Flat 4B, the week a product team is handed its meter trace

Flat 4B is a constructed household: a flat whose electricity meter writes one average-power reading every 15 minutes, 96 a day, for 14 days. A product team (constructed too, and unnamed) has been offered the trace and wants to build an "energy insights" feature on it. The account holder ticked a box. Nobody else who lives in, visits or works in the flat did. The team's analyst writes one threshold rule in an afternoon, and by evening the trace is telling her when the household wakes, leaves, comes back and goes to bed.

★ What you walk out with tonight. A clear picture of why the household, not a person, is the unit of home telemetry, and why that makes "the user" the wrong word. A measured case of inference without asking: on the constructed Flat 4B trace, one threshold on one meter reads 93.0 percent of 15-minute slots right and puts the leave time within 30 minutes on 12 of 14 days. LiveWhy this is the engine problem in a living room3 min▶

Harrow Lane's engines (real public rows from NASA's simulation, the degradation simulated with C-MAPSS) taught three absences. No author: a device writes the row and means nothing by it. No user id: the unit is an engine and a cycle count. No visible wrong row: a drifted reading looks exactly like a good one. Every one of the three holds in a home.

The meter at Flat 4B writes a number nobody typed. Its identity is a meter on a premises, not a person: whoever is home, the reading is the sum of all of them. And the number that matters, who is in and awake, is never in the data. It has to be inferred from a channel installed for another reason, the way the engine's health index had to be inferred from temperatures. What changes is who pays for a wrong or an unwanted inference. On an engine it is a hangar. In a home it is the people the trace describes.

Real world

The Flat 4B product team (constructed) opened the trace expecting a bill: kilowatt-hours by day. The analyst noticed that every weekday the readings drop to a low floor for most of the working day and climb again in the early evening. She did not need a model to see it; nobody would. That flat stretch is the household's absence, and the team had not asked for it, had not been told it would be there, and had no one to ask about it except the person who ticked the box.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • Household. Everyone whose life passes through one premises: residents, children, visitors, carers. The unit a home meter measures.
  • Account holder. The one person who signed for the service. Not the same thing as the household.
  • Telemetry. Readings a device sends on its own schedule, without anyone typing them. Here, one average-power figure every 15 minutes.
  • Occupancy. Whether someone is in, and here, in and awake. Never in the data; always inferred.
  • Inference. A fact about people derived from readings that do not state it. The trace says watts; the inference says "the flat was empty from 09:00".
  • Threshold rule. Flat 4B's whole method: a reading above the day's 10th percentile plus 120 W counts as someone up and at home.
  • Confounder. Something that moves the reading without the thing you infer: a timed appliance that runs with nobody home, a quiet hour when someone is in.
  • Constructed. Flat 4B and its product team are invented so that the truth is knowable. No figure describes a real household, meter, device or product.

LiveHow one rule prints a routine5 min▶

The rule has two parts and no model. Find the day's quiet floor: the 10th percentile of its 96 readings, which is the fridge and whatever sits on standby. Then call any reading more than 120 W above that floor "someone up and at home". The first such slot is when the household woke; the longest quiet run before 20:00 is when it was out; the last active slot is when it went to bed.

Across the 14 constructed days that rule reads 93.0 percent of the 15-minute slots right, against the truth the trace was built from. It puts the time the household left within 30 minutes on 12 of 14 days, and the median error on leave time is 0 minutes. A leader should hear that as a statement about the data, not about cleverness: the routine is so plainly in the trace that the crudest possible rule reads it.

Real world

In the Flat 4B team's review (constructed), the analyst shows day 1: woke 07:30, left 09:00, back 17:45, asleep 23:30. The account holder's flatmate is in the room as a test user. Her first question is not about accuracy. It is "who else can see that?"

LiveWhere the rule is wrong, and why that does not make it safe4 min▶

Flat 4B was built with two confounders. Some awake hours at home are quiet: someone reading, a laptop on battery. And on some days a timed appliance runs for three quarters of an hour with nobody in. The two leave times the rule got badly wrong, 225 minutes and 45 minutes off, both fall on days the timed appliance ran, but for two different reasons. On the 225-minute day the appliance broke the quiet stretch, so the rule saw a return that never happened. On the 45-minute day the flat simply went quiet for half an hour before anyone left, so the rule called the leave too early. Same rule, two failure modes, and only one of them is the timer.

It is tempting to treat those errors as protection. They are not. A wrong inference is still an inference about the household, and anything built on it (a reminder sent, a profile updated, a flag raised) acts on people who never saw it. Twelve days out of 14 were right to within half an hour; the errors add a second harm to the first rather than cancelling it.

Self-studyThe household is the unit, and it has no user id4 min▶

On an engine the unit is obvious: one engine, one serial, one failure. In a home the meter's identity is a premises. Its trace is the sum of everyone in it, and the people change: a partner moves in, a child grows up, a carer visits on weekdays, a lodger leaves. A product that calls the account holder "the user" quietly assumes that the person who signed is the person the data describes. Most of the time the trace describes several people, and most of them signed nothing.

The warehouse habit is to join on a customer id and treat the row as that customer's behaviour. In a home that join is a category error. The safer default for a leader is to write "the household" in every spec where the team wrote "the user", and to see which features still make sense. Part 2 · covers the privacy line

What the trace can be made to say, and about whom

11 min live

One trace can be read at several depths. At the bottom is what the contract was signed for: energy used, for the bill. Each rung above it is an inference, and each says more about the people and less about the electricity. The figure draws the ladder beside the household it describes, and draws the line where the bill ends.

LiveWhat researchers report a meter trace reveals4 min▶

Flat 4B measures two rungs: presence and routine. The literature on meter privacy reports more. A 2018 review by Giaconi, Gunduz and Poor, read at source, states that a third party holding smart-meter data may learn "a user's presence at home, her religious beliefs, disabilities, illnesses", and even which TV channel is on. Molina-Markham and colleagues' 2010 paper "Private Memoirs of a Smart Meter" is widely cited as showing activity inference from meter data; it is reported here, because its text could not be read.

None of the upper rungs is measured on this page, and Flat 4B was not built to carry them. The point for a leader is the direction of travel: every rung above the bill is an inference about people, the rungs get more sensitive as they go up, and each was made from the same trace the bill needs. The data does not change as you climb. Only what someone chooses to compute from it does.

Real world

The Flat 4B team's first feature idea (constructed) was "we noticed you are usually out from 09:00; switch the heating off then". It is helpful, it is on the third rung, and the same sentence, sent to the wrong inbox or kept in a leaked table, is the fourth: when the flat is empty.

LiveConsent from people who never signed up4 min▶

The account holder ticked a box. The partner, the child and the carer did not, and in most homes they never see the screen where the box was. A trace is a mixture: the meter cannot separate one person's kettle from another's, so an inference about "the household" is an inference about each of them. One person's tick cannot be stretched to cover what the team learns about the others, and the team usually cannot even list who the others are.

Whether that tick is a lawful basis for any particular inference is a legal question, and the answer depends on where the household is and what the product does. It is taught properly elsewhere: what counts as personal data and when the rules apply in learn GDPR, scope, and consent and its exemptions in learn PDPA and DNC, consent and exemptions. Nothing on this page is legal advice. The question a product team owns, before any lawyer is asked, is narrower: should we compute this at all?

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree4 min▶

Take the hardest case. An older person lives alone; a relative wants to know if the kettle has not gone on by mid-morning. The same third-rung inference that worried the flatmate in Part 1 is, here, the whole value of the product. One camp says an inference the household benefits from and has asked for is the point of home telemetry, and refusing it protects nobody. A second camp says the person in the home is rarely the one who asked, and that a check-in nobody can switch off is surveillance with a kind face. A third says the argument is in the wrong place: keep the data so coarse at the source (a daily total, not 15-minute readings) that the routine cannot be computed, and build only the features that survive.

Each camp is right about something. The first is right that "never infer occupancy" rules out real care. The second is right that the account holder and the person described are often different people. The third is right that a promise in code is weaker than an inference the data cannot support. The answer depends on who lives there, who asked, and who can say no. A leader cannot borrow it from another product; it has to be written down for this one.

Part 3 · build-along

Which inferences a product team should refuse to make

17 min hands on

No code. A decision exercise, done on paper for Flat 4B's product team and then for a device your organisation ships or is about to. Every inference the team proposes passes three gates in order, and any "no" at the first two or "yes" at the third sends it to the refuse list. The figure is the card to keep.

List what the trace could be made to say

For Flat 4B, write six candidate inferences the product team might propose, one per line: energy used this month; usage compared with last month; an appliance that seems to run constantly; someone is home right now; the household's weekday routine; the hours the flat is usually empty. Leave room to add your own. Do not rank them yet.

Gate 1: is it needed for what the household asked for?

Write the service in one sentence, the way the household would describe it ("tell us what we use and help us use less"). For each inference, ask whether that service breaks without it. "It would be a nice feature" is a no. Anything that fails goes to the refuse list with the words "not needed".

Gate 2: could every person in the home see it and say no?

Not the account holder: every person. Name them for Flat 4B (a flatmate, a partner, a visiting carer) and ask whether each could find out the inference exists and switch it off without asking anyone else. If the honest answer is "only the person who signed", it goes to the refuse list with the words "household not asked".

Gate 3: if it leaked, would it tell a stranger when the flat is empty?

Imagine the table the inference is stored in turns up somewhere it should not. If it lets a reader work out when nobody is home, or anything on the top rung of the ladder, refuse it, however useful it is. Write the reason next to it in one line.

For each refusal, ask whether the data could make it impossible

A refusal written in a policy can be undone by the next feature. A refusal built into the data cannot: if the product keeps only daily totals, no rule can read a leave time from it. For every inference on the refuse list, write down the coarsest reading that still serves the inferences you kept, and ask whether the team could keep only that.

Write the refuse list and get it signed

One page: the inferences the team will make, the ones it refuses and why, and the data resolution it will keep. Name who signed it. Then hand the inferences you kept, not the ones you refused, to whoever answers the legal question; that is the order that keeps the team's own judgement from being replaced by a yes from counsel.

The habit to keep. When a team shows you what a home device's data can do, do not ask whether the inference is accurate. Ask who it is about, whether all of them could say no, and what it would tell a stranger. Accuracy is the smallest of the three questions; Flat 4B's crudest rule was already right most of the time.

Check yourself

Three questions

A product lead says the Flat 4B trace is "only electricity readings, nothing about people". What is the right reading for a leader?

No model was needed: a two-part threshold rule read the routine off the constructed trace. The data already carries the inference; the choice is whether to compute it. The legal status is not decided here.

Flat 4B's account holder ticked a consent box. A flatmate and a visiting carer did not. Which statement fits the household as the unit?

The meter cannot tell one person's kettle from another's. Having no user id does not make a trace about nobody; it makes it about everyone at once. Whether a tick is a lawful basis is for the law sessions, not this page.

The two leave times the rule got badly wrong (225 and 45 minutes off) were both on days a timed appliance ran. What should a leader take from that?

A confounder that breaks the quiet stretch fools the rule on some days and not others. Accuracy and privacy are different questions: a more accurate model makes the inference more revealing, not less.

Before session 5

Homework

Twenty minutes, on one device in a home that your organisation ships, supports or buys data from.

Draw its ladder

At the bottom, what the household signed up for. Above it, every inference your product makes or could make from the same data, in rising order of what it says about the people. Draw the line where the service ends. Mark each rung "made today", "planned" or "possible".

Count the people the data describes

For a typical home using it, how many people does the data describe, and how many of them signed anything? If nobody on the team knows, that is the finding. Leader session 5 takes the same "no user id" idea back to the plant floor as one of five places the warehouse playbook breaks.

Run one rung through the three gates

Pick the highest rung marked "made today". Needed, all of them, if it leaked. Write the answer to each in one line, and bring it to the next session.
