Missing not at random, planted

NASA's FD001 file has no gaps at all, which makes it the perfect place to study them: you can add gaps on purpose, with a rule you write down first, and then measure exactly what the usual clean-up does. Tonight the rule is the sensor's worst habit. When the exhaust temperature runs hot, the reading vanishes. The pipeline then does what most cleaning scripts do on line one, drops every row with a gap, and the bench re-scores the best model on the same 300 cut points. The error most teams report barely moves. The score that prices lateness jumps. Real public rows from NASA's simulation, computed in your browser.

Part 0

Harrow Lane's feed starts losing its hottest readings

Harrow Lane Overhaul is a constructed engine-overhaul shop, and its fleet is the 100 simulated engines of NASA's FD001 training set. Leader session 2 showed its planning lead what dropped gaps do to a decision. Tonight its analyst rebuilds that result by hand: she writes the dropout rule, plants it with the engine, deletes the gappy rows the way her pipeline would, refits the bench's top rung and puts the two scores side by side. Then she asks the question deletion threw away: how long since the last reading?

★ What you walk out with tonight. A planted dropout you can state in one line: a row whose T50 reading is at or above 1416.2 degrees Rankine vanishes with probability 0.7, so 3,073 of 20,631 rows go. Why that is missing not at random in Rubin's sense, and why it lands at the end of life. The two-number move measured on 300 cut points: RMSE from 23.64 to 24.89, about 5 percent; the asymmetric score from 3,366 to 4,656, about 38 percent; the bias from 1.4 to 4.7 cycles late. A live check that the same number of rows removed at random does not do the same damage. And an honest list of what to do instead, with what this bench does and does not show about it.

LiveWhy a file with no gaps needs a plant3 min▶

On a real feed the reason behind each gap is unknown. You can argue that the probe overheats, or that the link saturates, or that a maintenance mode switches it off, and you can never be sure which one made a given hole. That uncertainty is why "missing not at random" usually stays an argument instead of a measurement.

FD001 removes the uncertainty from the other side. Every cycle of every engine is in the file, so the truth is complete. Plant the gaps with a rule written down before anything is measured, and the bench can compare the same model on the same 300 cut points with and without them. Nothing else changes, so the difference between the two scores is what the rule plus the clean-up did. The dropout is modelled, labelled modelled; the readings it removes are real public rows from NASA's simulation, in which the degradation itself is simulated (C-MAPSS).

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back3 min▶
  • The plant. A dropout rule applied on purpose to a complete file, seeded so the same rows vanish every run, declared on the widget.
  • Threshold. 1416.2 degrees Rankine: the 80th percentile of all 20,631 T50 readings, so the hottest fifth sits at or above it.
  • Missing completely at random. Whether a reading arrives has nothing to do with the engine or the reading.
  • Missing at random. Whether it arrives depends only on things you did record.
  • Missing not at random. Whether it arrives depends on the value that went missing. The three names are the shorthand of later texts, reported here.
  • Deletion. Dropping every row with a gap before modelling; also called complete-case analysis. The habit measured tonight.
  • Staleness. Cycles between the cut point and the newest row that survived. Zero when nothing vanished.
  • Top rung. The bench's best model: health index on 14 channels, label capped at 125, lag-free smoothing over the last 10 values.
  • Units under test. 300 cut points on 30 held-out engines (seed 2008), not the NASA test set; 70 engines fit the model.
Part 1 · covers the plant

A rule that reads temperature and removes old age

12 min live

The rule never looks at cycle numbers. It looks at one channel, T50 (the low-pressure turbine outlet temperature), and removes the row when that reading is hot. The figure splits all 20,631 rows into tenths of each engine's life and draws, for each tenth, how many rows are hot and how many of those the rule removed.

LiveThe rule, declared before anything is measured4 min▶

Three numbers define the plant, and the engine exposes all three as settings: the channel (mnarCh, s4, which is T50), the quantile that marks a reading as hot (mnarQuantile, 0.8), and the probability that a hot reading vanishes (mnarP, 0.7). The threshold is computed, not chosen: sort all 20,631 T50 readings and take the one 80 percent of the way up, 1416.2 degrees Rankine. Every hot row then gets a seeded random draw (seed 2008 plus 7), and 7 in 10 of them, on average, vanish. The count at the canon settings is 3,073 rows, 14.9 percent of the file.

Read the rule panel again: the row vanishes, not just the reading. A pipeline that drops rows with gaps throws away the other thirteen channels of every one of those cycles, all of which arrived intact. The plant reports a missing T50; the clean-up turns it into a missing cycle.

Real world

Harrow Lane's analyst (constructed, like the shop) writes the rule on the team's wiki before running anything, with the threshold, the probability and the seed. The reliability engineer objects that the shop's probes do not quit at exactly 1416.2. He is right, and it does not matter tonight: the point of a declared rule is that anyone can rerun it and get the same 3,073 rows, so an argument about the result is never an argument about which rows went.

LiveNot at random, in Rubin's sense4 min▶

Rubin (1976) asked when an analysis may ignore the process that caused some data to be missing. The abstract, read at source, gives two sets of conditions: for inference from sampling distributions, the data must be "missing at random" and "observed at random"; for likelihood or Bayesian inference, missing at random and the missingness parameter "distinct" from the one being estimated. It calls these "the weakest general conditions" under which ignoring that process is always appropriate.

The plant breaks the first condition by construction. Whether a T50 reading arrives depends on the value of that same reading, which is exactly the value you no longer hold once it is gone. That is the case later texts call missing not at random; the formal definitions and the three-way naming are in the full paper and in those texts, reported here, not read. Across all 100 engines it matters for remaining life: 1.6 percent of rows in the first half of life are hot, against 88.8 percent of rows in each engine's last 30 cycles. A rule about temperature is, in effect, a rule about age.

One honest nuance. The other thirteen channels also move as the engine nears failure, so what survives carries some information about what vanished. That is why the damage is measurable rather than total. It does not make the gap ignorable: the rule reads T50 itself, not anything you hold.

Self-studyWhat the plant imitates, and what it does not4 min▶

It imitates one specific failure of a device: electronics that cut out above a temperature, intermittently. The probability below 1 matters; a probe that always quit above the threshold would make every engine's end of life vanish completely, which is a different and easier problem to spot.

It does not imitate the other things a struggling feed does: repeat the last value, write a default, lose a burst of cycles to a saturated link, or quit for reasons tied to the operating condition rather than the reading. Each of those leaves a different shape of hole, and none is measured here. Leader session 2 lists them in its gap ledger; the bench measures only the one planted rule, followed by deletion.

Citation: Rubin, D. B. (1976). "Inference and Missing Data." Biometrika 63(3), 581-592. DOI 10.1093/biomet/63.3.581. Abstract read at source.

Part 2 · covers deletion and the two-number move

Drop the gaps, and the estimates slide late

12 min live

Deletion reaches the model in two places: it thins the end of life out of the training engines, and at every cut point it hands the model an older row as if it were today. The figure draws both, then where the errors land on the asymmetric score's curve and what the two headline numbers say about it.

LiveReading the widget4 min▶

At 0.7 the first two rows are the canon figures: every reading arrives, RMSE 23.64 and summed score 3,366; hot readings vanish and the gappy rows are dropped, RMSE 24.89 and score 4,656, with the bias moving from 1.4 to 4.7 cycles late. Now look at the third row. It removes about the same number of rows, chosen at random from the whole file, and then drops them the same way. Its numbers stay close to the clean row and its bias barely moves.

That third row is the whole session in one line. The size of the hole is about the same in rows two and three; only the reason differs. Deletion is roughly harmless when the reason has nothing to do with the engine and expensive when the reason is the reading itself. The at-random row is exploration, never a canon figure, and it holds for this model at this seed; drag the slider and watch the bias column, which keeps growing for the hot rule and stays nearly flat for the random one. Other probabilities are not canon settings: your numbers will differ from the figures in the prose.

LiveThe two-number move4 min▶

RMSE goes up by 1.25, about 5 percent. A team that reports only square error would file the dropout under rounding. The PHM08 score goes up by 1,290, about 38 percent. The difference is not that one number is right. Square error treats a miss of 5 cycles early and 5 late the same and grows with the square of the miss; the score grows exponentially and divides a late miss by 10 but an early one by 13, so in the paper's words "late predictions were more heavily penalized than early predictions". A modest shift of the whole error distribution towards late is almost invisible to the first and expensive in the second.

Does it hold beyond seed 2008? On every seed tried (2008, 1, 7 and 42), the dropout rung is worse than the top rung on both numbers and later in bias. The size moves a lot: seed 42 goes from 22.32 / 2,562 to 23.16 / 2,993, seed 7 from 24.82 / 4,891 to 26.25 / 7,351 (RMSE / summed score). Quote the direction; quote a size only with its seed.

Real world

Harrow Lane's model review (constructed) has one slide of metrics, and it is RMSE. When the analyst adds the bias and the summed score beside it, the planning lead's first question is not about the model at all: "Which step in the pipeline made us late?" The answer is the one-line clean-up at the top of the feature script. It had been there for two years and nobody had ever measured it, because the number on the slide could not see it.

LiveWhat to do instead, and what this bench does not show3 min▶

Three habits follow from the mechanism. Never drop silently: if a step removes rows, it logs how many and from where in each unit's life, so the shape of the hole is visible before any model is fitted. Flag the gap: keep the row, mark the missing channel, and keep the other thirteen readings that did arrive. Keep the gap as a feature: cycles since the last reading of each channel is itself a reading, and under this plant it is a hot-engine detector. The build-along measures that last point directly: near failure the newest surviving row is about two cycles stale; far from failure it is almost never stale.

Now the honest part. The bench measures deletion only. It does not fit a model with a gap flag or a staleness feature, and it does not test any fill method, so this page makes no claim that any of them recovers the 1,290. Any fill computed only from what survived rests on the same assumption deletion does, that what you hold explains the gap, and under a rule that reads the missing value that assumption fails. Imputation methods and missingness as a model input are taught in learn feature engineering, practitioner session 7, missing data as signal; the second homework below lets you try one here, with no canon number to aim at.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree3 min▶

One score or several? The paper that published this data ranked the 2008 challenge on one summed asymmetric score, and in the same paper its authors argued for more than one number: weighting short true lives more heavily and adding a correlation measure, because a summed score can hide uneven performance across units. Tonight is a case for several numbers: RMSE alone hid the slide, and the score alone would not have told you it was a slide (the bias did).

The other side is just as practical: a bench with four numbers lets a team quote whichever one flatters the change. The bench prints RMSE, the summed score, the bias and the late share side by side and crowns none of them. Practitioner session 7 takes the score apart, including a printed equation and a figure in the original paper that disagree about which side it punishes harder.

One more modelling choice sits under every number here: the label is capped at 125 cycles, a convention chosen on this subset (130 is also in use, and the bench prints it). The top rung's smoothing is a lag-free local line over the last 10 values; its gain is small, 0.36 of RMSE, and a trailing mean of the same window lags and makes both numbers worse (24.88 / 4,171). Neither smoothing is a fix for dropout.

Part 3 · build-along

Plant it, drop it, re-score it, then read the gap

18 min hands on

Open the browser console on this page (the sample and the engine are already loaded). Six steps, each with the number you should see at the canon settings. The figure is the order of operations; the only lossy step is the copper one, and you will know exactly which rows it took.

Find the line

Decode the fleet, collect every T50 reading (channel s4), sort them and take the one 80 percent of the way up. You should see 20,631 readings and a threshold of 1416.2 degrees Rankine.

consolevar units = IOT.decode(IOT_SAMPLE); var k = IOT_SAMPLE.channels.indexOf("s4"); var t50 = []; units.forEach(function (u) { u.rows.forEach(function (r) { t50.push(r[k]); }); }); t50.sort(function (a, b) { return a - b; }); [t50.length, t50[Math.floor(0.8 * (t50.length - 1))].toFixed(1)]

Plant the dropout

The engine's own planting function, at its defaults: channel s4, quantile 0.8, probability 0.7, seed 2008. It returns the surviving rows of every engine and a receipt. You should see 3,073 of 20,631 rows gone, 14.9 percent.

consolevar p = IOT.prepare(units, IOT.DEFAULTS, "mnar", IOT_SAMPLE.channels); [p.info.dropped, p.info.total, (100 * p.info.dropped / p.info.total).toFixed(1)]

See where the holes landed

Count the vanished rows in each engine's last 30 cycles and in its first half. You should see 1,868 of 3,000 late rows gone against 115 of 10,290 early ones. The rule never read the cycle number; the age pattern came from the heat.

consolevar late = 0, nLate = 0, early = 0, nEarly = 0; units.forEach(function (u, i) { var kept = {}; p.units[i].cyc.forEach(function (c) { kept[c] = 1; }); u.cyc.forEach(function (c) { if (c > u.n - 30) { nLate++; if (!kept[c]) late++; } else if (c <= u.n / 2) { nEarly++; if (!kept[c]) early++; } }); }); [late, nLate, early, nEarly]

Score the top rung, with and without the drop

The ladder can compute just the two rungs you need. The second one plants the same dropout, drops the rows, refits on the 70 training engines and scores the same 300 cut points. You should see 23.64, 3,366, +1.4, 56% and then 24.89, 4,656, +4.7, 60%.

consolevar r = IOT.ladder(IOT_SAMPLE, { only: ["smooth", "mnarSmooth"] }); [r.smooth, r.mnarSmooth].map(function (x) { return [x.rmse.toFixed(2), Math.round(x.score), x.bias.toFixed(1), Math.round(100 * x.lateShare) + "%"]; })

Make the two-number move

Put the changes side by side, in units and in percent. You should see RMSE up 1.25 (5%) and the summed score up 1,290 (38%). Write both in your notes, with the bias, and with "n = 300 cut points on 30 held-out engines, not the NASA test set".

consolevar a = r.smooth, b = r.mnarSmooth; [(b.rmse - a.rmse).toFixed(2), Math.round(100 * (b.rmse / a.rmse - 1)) + "%", Math.round(b.score - a.score), Math.round(100 * (b.score / a.score - 1)) + "%"]

Read the gap as a reading

At every cut point, how many cycles separate today from the newest row that survived? Split the 300 cuts by true remaining life. You should see 47 cuts with 30 or fewer cycles left averaging 2.19 cycles stale, and 93 cuts with more than 100 left averaging 0.01. The gap knows the engine is hot even though the hot reading is gone.

consolevar near = [], far = []; r.cuts.forEach(function (c) { var last = 0; p.units[c.u - 1].cyc.forEach(function (x) { if (x <= c.cut) last = x; }); if (c.rul <= 30) near.push(c.cut - last); if (c.rul > 100) far.push(c.cut - last); }); function avg(v) { return v.reduce(function (s, x) { return s + x; }, 0) / v.length; } [near.length, avg(near).toFixed(2), far.length, avg(far).toFixed(2)]

The habit to keep. Before any clean-up step ships, plant its worst plausible reason on a complete copy of the data, run the step, and report two numbers and the bias. If the step removes rows, log where in each unit's life they came from. A gap is a reading about the device; delete it and you delete the reading.

Check yourself

Three questions

Which property of the planted rule makes the dropout missing not at random, in Rubin's sense?

Size is not the test, and a seeded draw is still a random draw. Rubin's weakest general conditions for ignoring the missing-data process start from missing at random; a rule that reads the missing value itself is outside them, and because heat lives at the end of life, the holes land there.

After the plant and the drop, RMSE moves 23.64 to 24.89 and the summed score 3,366 to 4,656, on 300 cut points. Your review deck shows only RMSE. What do you add beside it?

Both runs use the same 300 cut points and the same model, so the difference is the drop. On every seed tried the dropout rung is worse on both numbers and later in bias. A late slide of a few cycles adds little to squared error and a lot to a score that divides a late miss by 10 and an early one by 13.

In the widget, removing about the same number of rows at random barely moves the bias, while removing the hot ones pushes it late. What does that show?

The comparison holds the count fixed and changes only the reason, so the difference is the reason. "Always safe" overreaches: the at-random row is exploration on one model at one seed, not a law, and it is not a canon figure.

Before session 7

Homework

Forty minutes, in the console on this page. Neither of the first two has a canon number; write down what you get and the seed you got it on.

Split the damage in two

Deletion reached the model in two places, the fit and the input. Build one units array with the clean training engines and the planted held-out engines, and another the other way round (r.testIds lists the 30 held-out engines; the other 70 are trainIds), then score each with IOT.runRung(mixed, trainIds, r.cuts, r.opts, "smooth"). Which half moves the summed score more? Bring the answer to session 7, which asks why a score can move more than its error.

Try the gap as a feature

After the plant, add a fifteenth input to every surviving row: cycles since the previous surviving row of that engine. Append it to each row of p.units and score the augmented units with IOT.runRung exactly as in the first homework; the engine standardises and fits whatever columns it is given. If it helps, check seeds 1, 7 and 42 before believing it; one seed is an anecdote.

Find the line that drops rows in a pipeline you own

Search one feature script for the step that removes rows with gaps. Write next to it: what the device does when it struggles, where in a unit's life those gaps would land, and which of your reported numbers would show a late slide.
