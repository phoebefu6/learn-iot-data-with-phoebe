Smart home telemetry: occupancy and where to stop

Eight sessions on engines taught you to read a sensor row that nobody typed. Tonight the sensor is an electricity meter in a home, and the row is about people. You build the occupancy rule yourself on Flat 4B, a constructed household, reproduce how well it reads the household, and then do the thing a data team rarely measures: blur the trace on purpose and see how much of the routine survives. The answers are design choices with a cost, not a compliance checklist. Nothing here is legal advice; the law lives in the GDPR and PDPA sessions linked below.

Part 0

Flat 4B's trace lands on a practitioner's desk

Flat 4B is a constructed household: its electricity meter writes one average-power reading every 15 minutes, 96 a day, for 14 days, 1,344 readings in all. Because it is constructed, the truth is known: for every slot, whether someone was awake at home. Leader session 4 asked which inferences a product team should refuse to make. Tonight the practitioner who has to build or refuse them gets the trace, writes the rule, checks it against the truth, and measures what each way of stopping actually stops.

★ What you walk out with tonight. An occupancy detector you wrote yourself in a dozen lines, matching the engine's reading of every one of the 1,344 slots and reproducing its numbers: 93.0 percent of slots right, leave time within 30 minutes on 12 of 14 days, median error 0 minutes. The mechanism behind its two bad misses, both on days a timed appliance ran. A clear account of why the household is not a user, and whose consent a meter trace never had. And a measured answer to "will coarsening protect them?": on this trace, averaging to an hour hides almost nothing, four hours hides the leave time on most days, and a daily total reads no better than guessing. Each stop priced in what the product loses. Constructed data throughout; your numbers will differ on another household.

LiveThe engine problem, on a smaller wire3 min▶

Harrow Lane Overhaul (constructed; its fleet is real public rows from NASA's simulation, the degradation simulated with C-MAPSS) taught that the quantity you care about is never a column. Engine health had to be inferred from temperatures; remaining life had to be computed from a failure that arrived once, at the end. A home meter is the same shape. The column is watts. The quantity a product wants, who is in and awake, is never written down, and has to be inferred from a channel installed to send a bill.

Two things change. The unit is not a machine but a household: whoever is home, the reading is all of them added together. And a wrong or unwanted inference is not paid for by a hangar but by the people the trace describes, most of whom never signed anything. That is why "where to stop" is a practitioner's question too, not only a leader's: the stop is built in the pipeline, at a resolution, on a device or in a table.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back3 min▶
  • Slot. One 15-minute interval. 96 per day; slot 36 starts at 09:00.
  • Average power. What the meter writes for each slot, in watts: the energy used in the slot divided by its length.
  • Floor. The day's 10th percentile reading: the fridge and whatever sits on standby.
  • Threshold rule. A reading more than 120 W above the day's floor counts as someone up and at home.
  • Leave time. The start of the longest quiet run between the first active slot and 20:00.
  • Confounder. Something that moves the reading without the thing you infer: a quiet awake hour, or a timed appliance running in an empty flat.
  • Coarsening. Keeping fewer, longer readings: hourly or daily averages instead of every 15 minutes.
  • Constructed. Flat 4B and its product team are invented so that the truth is knowable. No figure describes a real household, meter, device or product.
Part 1 · covers the trace, the threshold and what it reveals

One threshold reads the household

12 min live

The rule has no model in it: a floor, a margin and a run-length. The figure draws it on the day it fails worst, because the failure shows the mechanism better than a good day does. Day 8 is a weekday; the household woke at 06:30, left at 07:45 and came back at 18:30. At 10:45 a timed appliance ran for three quarters of an hour in an empty flat.

LiveThe rule, and how well it reads the household4 min▶

Three lines. Sort the day's 96 readings and take the 10th percentile as the floor: the fridge and the standby load, whatever is on when nobody is doing anything. Call any reading more than 120 W above that floor "someone up and at home". Then read the routine off the on and off slots: the first active slot is waking, the longest quiet run before 20:00 is the absence, the last active slot is bed.

Checked against the truth the trace was built from, across all 14 constructed days, the rule reads 93.0 percent of the 15-minute slots right. It puts the leave time within 30 minutes on 12 of 14 days, and the median leave-time error is 0 minutes. The floor is per day, so it adapts to a day with the heating on or a weekend with more cooking; the margin is fixed. You will rebuild all of it in the build-along and get the same numbers to the slot.

Real world

The Flat 4B team's practitioner (constructed) was asked for "a quick look at whether the trace is useful". She wrote these three lines before lunch, printed day 1 (woke 07:30, left 09:00, back 17:45, asleep 23:30; the truth has the return at 17:30) and realised the quick look was already a schedule of when the flat stands empty. Nobody had asked her to compute that. Nobody had asked her not to.

LiveWhere it slips, mechanism by mechanism4 min▶

Flat 4B was built with two confounders, and the rule's errors split cleanly between them. Of the 94 slots it reads wrong, 85 are quiet awake slots: someone at home, reading or on a laptop, drawing less than the margin. Only 9 are the opposite, an appliance on with nobody in. You will count both in the build-along.

The two bad leave-time misses both fall on days the timed appliance ran, but they fail differently. Day 8 is the figure: the timer at 10:45 cut the empty stretch into a short half and a long half, and the rule took the long one, 225 minutes late. Day 9 failed 45 minutes early: the household's last half hour before leaving was quiet, so the rule saw them go at 07:45 when they left at 08:30, and the timer at 12:45 split the empty day into two halves of equal length, so the rule kept the first. Say both mechanisms; "the timer did it" is only half true.

Self-studyWhat the trace says beyond occupancy4 min▶

Flat 4B measures presence and routine, and from them, read backwards, the hours the flat is empty. It was not built to carry more, so nothing more is measured here. The research literature reports more. Giaconi, Gunduz and Poor (2018), read at source, state that a third party with smart-meter data may learn "a user's presence at home, her religious beliefs, disabilities, illnesses", and even the TV channel being watched. Molina-Markham and colleagues' 2010 paper "Private Memoirs of a Smart Meter" is widely cited as showing activity inference from meter data; it is reported here, because its text could not be read.

For a practitioner the lesson is about the pipeline, not the paper. Every one of those inferences is computed from the same 15-minute column the bill needs. Nothing in the data changes as the inference climbs from "energy used" to "when nobody is home"; only the code someone chooses to run on it does. That is why the stops in Part 2 act on the data itself, where they can make an inference impossible rather than merely unwritten.

Part 2 · covers consent, the household as the unit and where to stop

Where to stop, and what each stop costs

12 min live

A data team can stop an inference in three places on the path from the meter to a feature: at the source, by keeping coarser readings; on a device in the home, by running the rule there and sending only the answer; or before storage, by keeping a building's total instead of each flat's trace. LiveThe household is not a user4 min▶

Every warehouse habit says to join the row to a customer id and treat it as that customer's behaviour. A meter has no such id. Its identity is a premises, and its reading is the sum of everyone inside: a partner, a child, a lodger, a carer who visits on weekdays. When the rule says "left at 09:00", it is a statement about each of them at once, and the meter cannot say which.

The consent problem follows directly. One account holder ticked a box; the others never saw the screen it was on, and the team usually cannot even list who they are. One person's tick cannot be stretched over what the team learns about the rest. Whether that tick is a lawful basis for a given inference is a legal question that depends on where the household is and what the product does: what counts as personal data is taught in learn GDPR, scope, and consent and its exemptions in learn PDPA and DNC, consent and exemptions. Nothing here is legal advice. What a practitioner owns is narrower and earlier: whether the pipeline can compute the inference at all.

Real world

The Flat 4B team (constructed) rewrote its spec, replacing every "the user" with "the household". Three features survived unchanged (monthly usage, usage against last month, a device that never switches off). Two did not: "we noticed you are out from 09:00" and "home-again alerts". Nobody had to argue about them; the rewrite made it obvious who they were about.

LiveThree stops, each with a price5 min▶

Coarsen at the source. Keep fewer, longer readings. Set the panel above to 1 hour and the surprise is how little it buys: the rule still reads 92.9 percent of slots right and the leave time within 30 minutes on 13 of 14 days, one more than at 15 minutes, because averaging four readings smooths away some quiet awake slots. At 30 minutes it reads more slots right than the original, 94.9 percent. Coarsening is not a privacy dial that turns smoothly; on this trace it does nothing until it is coarse enough to swallow the absence itself. At 4 hours the leave time is within 30 minutes on only 2 of 14 days. A daily total reads 68.2 percent of slots right, exactly what guessing "out" for every slot scores. The price: anything that needs the shape of a day, such as time-of-use advice or a fault that shows only as a short spike, goes with it.

Infer on the device. Run the rule in the home and send only its answer ("the heating could switch off at 09:00 on weekdays") plus a daily total. The 15-minute trace never crosses the wall. The price is real: compute and updates on a device, a rule nobody outside can re-run or audit, and a harder time finding out when it is wrong. Whether that compute and the link can carry it is a budget, and practitioner session 8 builds that budget.

Aggregate before storing. Keep a building's total, not each flat's trace. Per-flat features die, by design. How many households make an aggregate safe is not measured here and no number is quoted; what is known is that a small group can be unpicked, so "aggregate" without a minimum group size is a hope, not a stop.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree4 min▶

Three positions are held by people who have thought hard about it. The first says data minimisation is the stop: if the pipeline never holds 15-minute readings, no later feature, leak or request can compute a routine from them, and a promise in code is weaker than an inference the data cannot support. The second says that is too blunt: coarse data kills useful features, the household may want them, and the right stop is computation that stays in the home, with the household able to see and switch off each inference. The third says neither is enough on its own, because both are decided by the team; what matters is that every person in the home can learn what is inferred and say no, and the technical stop is secondary.

There is a measurement argument underneath too. The panel scores privacy by how well an attacker's rule still works, and that depends on the rule. A cleverer detector might read a 4-hour trace better than this threshold does, so "2 of 14" is an upper bound on what coarsening hides only for this rule, not a guarantee. Some argue privacy should be judged against the best possible inference; others say that is unknowable and a practical test against a realistic one is the only number a team can act on. Each side is right about something; the choice belongs to the team that has to sign the design.

Part 3 · build-along

Build the rule, reproduce it, then blur the trace

18 min hands on

Open the browser console on this page (the engine is already loaded). Six steps, each with the number you should see. You write the detector yourself, prove it matches the engine slot for slot, then coarsen the trace and measure how fast the inference degrades. The figure is the result you are about to compute, drawn for one day.

Build the household

IOT.household builds Flat 4B's constructed trace from a seed; 909 is the canon. It returns the watts, the built-in truth, the plan of each day and the engine's own inference, which you are about to reproduce without looking at. You should see 14 days, 96 slots a day, 1,344 readings, and day 1 is a weekday.

consolevar h = IOT.household(909); [h.days, h.slotsPerDay, h.watts.length, h.plan[0].weekend]

Write the threshold detector

For each day, sort its 96 readings and take the one at index 9 (the 10th percentile: 0.1 times 95, rounded down) as the floor; a reading above floor plus 120 W is "up and at home". Then check it against the engine's inference and the truth. You should see true (every slot matches the engine), 1,250 slots right, and "93.0".

consolefunction detect(w) { var out = []; for (var d = 0; d < 14; d++) { var day = w.slice(d * 96, d * 96 + 96); var floor = day.slice().sort(function (a, b) { return a - b; })[9]; day.forEach(function (x) { out.push(x > floor + 120 ? 1 : 0); }); } return out; } var mine = detect(h.watts); var same = mine.every(function (v, i) { return v === h.inferred[i]; }); var hit = mine.filter(function (v, i) { return v === h.truth[i]; }).length; [same, hit, (100 * hit / 1344).toFixed(1)]

Read the leave time and reproduce the canon

Leave time is the start of the longest quiet run after the first active slot and before slot 80 (20:00). Score it against each day's plan in minutes. You should see "0 15 0 0 0 0 0 225 45 0 0 15 0 0" and 12: twelve days within 30 minutes, median error 0, and the two bad misses on days 8 and 9.

consolefunction leave(inf, d) { var first = -1, best = 0, bestStart = -1, run = 0, runStart = -1; for (var s = 0; s < 80; s++) { var a = inf[d * 96 + s]; if (a && first < 0) first = s; if (first < 0) continue; if (!a) { if (run === 0) runStart = s; run++; if (run > best) { best = run; bestStart = runStart; } } else run = 0; } return bestStart; } var err = h.plan.map(function (p, d) { return Math.abs(leave(mine, d) - p.leave) * 15; }); [err.join(" "), err.filter(function (e) { return e <= 30; }).length]

Split the errors by mechanism

Count the two kinds of wrong slot: awake at home but read as out (quiet hours), and out but read as home (the timer). Then read when the timer ran on the two bad days. You should see 85 quiet slots missed, 9 phantom slots, and timer starts at slot 43 (10:45) and slot 51 (12:45).

consolevar missed = 0, phantom = 0; mine.forEach(function (v, i) { if (h.truth[i] && !v) missed++; if (!h.truth[i] && v) phantom++; }); [missed, phantom, h.plan[7].timer, h.plan[8].timer]

Coarsen the trace and rerun the rule

Average each block of k readings and repeat the average k times, so the blurred trace still has one value per slot and the same detector and leave reader run unchanged. You should see 93.0 / 12 at 15 minutes, 94.9 / 12 at 30, 92.9 / 13 at 60, 87.9 / 6 at 120, 82.5 / 2 at 240, 81.2 / 0 at 480 and 68.2 / 0 at a day. The live panel in Part 2 runs the same code.

consolefunction coarsen(w, k) { var out = []; for (var i = 0; i < w.length; i += k) { var m = 0; for (var j = 0; j < k; j++) m += w[i + j]; for (j = 0; j < k; j++) out.push(m / k); } return out; } [1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 96].map(function (k) { var inf = detect(coarsen(h.watts, k)), ok = 0, near = 0; inf.forEach(function (v, i) { if (v === h.truth[i]) ok++; }); h.plan.forEach(function (p, d) { var l = leave(inf, d); if (l >= 0 && Math.abs(l - p.leave) * 15 <= 30) near++; }); return k * 15 + " min: " + (100 * ok / 1344).toFixed(1) + "%, " + near + " of 14"; })

Score against knowing nothing

A slot score means nothing without its floor. Guess "out" for every slot and count how often that is right. You should see 917 slots and "68.2": the daily total scores exactly that, so it reads no better than knowing nothing, while the 4-hour trace's 82.5 sits well above it. Write one line: the resolution at which the leave time stops being readable, and what the product loses there.

consolevar out = h.truth.filter(function (t) { return t === 0; }).length; [out, (100 * out / 1344).toFixed(1)]

The habit to keep. Before you call a step a privacy protection, attack it with the simplest inference you fear and score it against knowing nothing. On Flat 4B, the hourly trace looked like a protection and was not; the four-hour trace was, for this rule, and only the measurement could tell them apart.

Check yourself

Three questions

A teammate proposes keeping hourly averages instead of 15-minute readings "so we can't see when people are home". On the constructed Flat 4B trace, what does the measurement say?

The absence lasts hours; an hourly average cannot hide something longer than itself. Averaging even smoothed some quiet awake slots, which is why the hourly leave time is within 30 minutes on one more day. The leave time only went on most days at 4 hours.

At 4-hour resolution the rule still reads 82.5 percent of slots right. How should a practitioner read that number?

A slot score is inflated by the many easy night and empty-day slots. The inference that matters, when the flat stands empty, mostly disappears at 4 hours. And the measurement holds for this rule only; a cleverer detector could read more.

The Flat 4B team decides to run the rule on a device in the home and send only its answer and a daily total. Which statement is right?

On-device inference moves where the trace lives; it does not change who the inference is about. The household is not a user, and one tick does not stretch over the others. The legal question is not answered here.

Before session 10

Homework

Thirty minutes, in the console on this page and on paper.

Try another household

Run your detect, leave and coarsen functions on IOT.household(1) and IOT.household(42). These are exploration, not canon: your numbers will differ from Flat 4B's. Find the coarsest resolution at which the leave time is still within 30 minutes on at least half the days, for each seed. Does the hour that failed to protect Flat 4B fail on the others too?

Make the rule smarter, then re-attack

Change one thing in the detector (the margin, the percentile, or a rule that ignores a raised stretch shorter than an hour) and rerun the coarsening ladder. If your rule reads the 4-hour trace better than 2 of 14, write down what that says about trusting a privacy number measured with one attacker.

For a home device your organisation ships, supports or buys data from: the resolution it keeps, where its inferences run, what is stored per home, and the price of each choice in features lost.
