The asymmetric score

LiveWhy a score needs a direction3 min▶

A remaining-life estimate can be wrong in two ways. Too early, and an engine with life left is pulled: a false alarm, a waiting line, life thrown away. Too late, and the engine stays in service past its end. Leader session 3 priced those two mistakes for the people who sign off a model. Tonight is the practitioner's half: the formula itself, read carefully enough to implement it and to catch an implementation that leans the wrong way.

RMSE cannot tell those two mistakes apart. It squares every miss, so 20 cycles early and 20 cycles late weigh exactly the same. The asymmetric score was built to tell them apart, and that is both its value and its trap: a number with a direction is only as trustworthy as your knowledge of which direction it points.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back3 min▶
  • d. The estimated remaining life minus the true one, in cycles. Below zero is early; zero or above is late.
  • Unit under test. One engine at one cut point. The bench has 300: 10 seeded cut points on each of 30 held-out engines (seed 2008), not the NASA test set.
  • Unit score. The cost of one unit's miss: e raised to the size of d over a constant, minus 1. Zero for a perfect estimate.
  • Summed score. The unit scores added over all 300 units. Smaller is better. The bench calls it "score".
  • The two constants. 13 and 10. Whichever side divides by 10 climbs faster and is the side punished harder.
  • Orientation, or lean. Which side gets which constant. The bench follows the paper's figure and prose: early divides by 13, late by 10.
  • Bias. The mean of d over the 300 units. Positive means the estimates lean late.
  • Late share. The share of units whose estimate lands after the truth.
  • Constructed. Harrow Lane is invented so the truth is knowable. The engine rows are NASA's simulation; no figure describes a real engine.
Part 1 · covers the formula and its printed orientation

One formula, two readings

12 min live

The score was published with the data by Saxena, Goebel, Simon and Eklund (2008), in the paper that ships inside NASA's zip. Its section VII defines it, plots it and explains it. Those three statements of the same score do not all agree on which side is punished harder. The figure draws one unit's cost against its miss under both readings, and lists the three places the paper states the lean.

LiveThe formula, term by term4 min▶

For each unit, take d: the estimated remaining life minus the true remaining life, in cycles. If d is below zero the estimate is early, and the unit costs e raised to the power of minus d over 13, minus 1. If d is zero or above the estimate is late, and the unit costs e raised to d over 10, minus 1. Add the unit costs over every unit under test. That sum is the score; smaller is better.

Each piece earns its place. The exponential makes the penalty grow faster than the miss: in the paper's words, "the penalty grows exponentially with increasing error". The minus 1 makes a perfect estimate cost exactly 0. The two constants set the lean: dividing by the smaller number makes that side climb faster. With early over 13 and late over 10, a miss of 20 cycles costs 6.39 late and 3.66 early; a miss of 50 costs 147.41 late and 45.81 early, about three times as much. The paper states the intent plainly: "late predictions were more heavily penalized than early predictions".

Two consequences follow before any model is fitted. The score has units of nothing (it is not cycles, and it is not money). And because it is a sum of exponentials, it is ruled by its worst few units, which Part 2 measures.

LiveThe printed equation against the figure4 min▶

Read the paper's equation (11) on its own and you find a1 = 10 paired with d < 0 and a2 = 13 paired with d >= 0. Taken literally, the early side divides by the smaller number, climbs faster, and is punished harder. That is the dashed curve in the figure.

Now read Figure 9, the plotted curve, off its axes: about 46 at d = -50 and about 147 at d = +50. Compute both candidates at that point and only one matches: early over 13 gives 45.81, late over 10 gives 147.41. The prose of the same section says late predictions were penalized more heavily. So the printed pairing and the figure disagree, and the figure matches the stated intent. The bench follows the figure and the prose, and its self-check "late costs more than early at equal size" holds it there.

The reading lesson is not about this one paper. A formula in a PDF, a plot of it and a sentence describing it are three statements of the same thing. When they disagree, compute a value you can read off the plot and see which statement it agrees with. Then write down which reading you followed. Nothing on this page can say what code the authors ran in 2008; only what the page shows.

Real world

Harrow Lane's analyst (constructed, like the shop) had copied a scoring function from a public code notebook, and its tests all passed, because the tests had been written from the printed equation. She ran one line, the function at d = +20 and at d = -20, and the early miss came back more expensive. She did not quietly "fix" it. She re-ran the ladder under both readings, found that the rung she was about to recommend changed between them, and added a line to the review naming which reading every score on the slide followed.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree4 min▶

Which way should the score lean? For engines the paper prefers early to late, and its constants encode that. The same section also says that "in specific situations" where failures are not life-threatening, early predictions "may instead involve significant economic burden", and that the preference can be tuned. So the score's own authors hold both positions for different machines. The constants are a business choice; leader session 3 is about making it.

Which way does the score lean on paper? The printed equation and the figure disagree, as above. Every later paper and code repository you meet may have picked either one, usually without saying so. The bench follows the figure and the prose because they agree with each other and with the stated intent. That is a reason, not a ruling: if you meet a published result scored the other way, it is not wrong, it is a different score, and its numbers are not comparable with these.

One summed score or several? The PHM08 challenge ranked entries on the one sum. The same paper argues for weighting units with a short true remaining life more heavily, and for a correlation measure beside the sum, because a summed score can hide uneven performance across units. The bench prints RMSE, the summed score, the bias and the late share, and crowns none.

LiveReading the lean widget4 min▶

Start with the slider. At d = +50 the published lean charges 147.41 and the swapped one 45.81; drag to -50 and the two trade places. That is the whole difference between the two readings, for one unit. Then read the table: every rung, its RMSE and its rank by RMSE, its summed score and its rank by score, its bias and its late share.

Under the published lean, look at rung 2 and the cap-130 convention check: RMSE ranks the cap-130 rung ahead (23.70 against 24.00), the score ranks rung 2 ahead (3,542 against 3,622). Look at the trailing-mean contrast and the planted dropout: RMSE calls them a tie (24.88 against 24.89), the score puts them well apart (4,171 against 4,656). Now switch the lean. The RMSE column does not change, because RMSE has no side. The score column reorders, and the rung at the top is no longer rung 3.

LiveWhy the baseline scores 305,1674 min▶

The mean-life baseline knows one number: the 70 training engines lived 200.37 cycles on average. At every cut point it predicts 200.37 minus the cycles already run, floored at zero. Two of the 30 held-out engines outlive that average by a long way: engine 69 (362 cycles, the longest in FD001) and engine 96 (336). At their cut points they are already past cycle 200, so the baseline predicts 0 to 14 cycles left when 140 to 150 remain.

Those misses land on the early side, the side divided by 13, and the exponential still makes them enormous. The single worst unit (d = -142) costs 55,440 on its own, 18.2 percent of the sum. Six units from those two engines carry 79.5 percent. A sum of exponentials is ruled by its worst few units; for contrast, rung 3's three worst units carry 12.8 percent of its sum. Note the irony: the baseline leans early (bias -12.1 cycles, 43 percent late), so its catastrophe sits on the gentle side. Swap the constants in the widget and that side becomes the steep one, and the same two engines make the baseline's sum many times larger again.

LiveThe ladder through both lenses4 min▶

Every rung, published lean, 300 cut points on 30 held-out engines (not the NASA test set), as RMSE / summed score, with bias:

  • Rung 0, mean-life baseline. 45.61 / 305,167, bias -12.1, 43 percent late. Mostly two long-lived engines.
  • Rung 1, linear fit on 14 raw channels, uncapped target. 30.50 / 28,557, bias +15.8, 70 percent late.
  • Rung 2, health index with a target capped at 125. 24.00 / 3,542, bias +0.8. The gain over rung 1 comes from capping the target and clamping, not from folding the channels into one number. The cap is a convention; cap 130 on rung 3 gives 23.70 / 3,622.
  • Rung 3, plus lag-free smoothing. 23.64 / 3,366, bias +1.4. A small, real gain (0.36 of RMSE); a trailing mean of the same window lags and makes both numbers worse, 24.88 / 4,171.
  • Anti-lever, hottest readings missing and gaps dropped. 24.89 / 4,656, bias +4.7.
  • Break, cycle order shuffled. 40.41 / 46,145.

The two-number headline: the planted dropout moves RMSE by only +1.25 (5 percent) but the score by +1,290 (38 percent), and the bias goes late. The square-error number barely notices a late shift that the asymmetric score prices. And between rung 1 and rung 3, RMSE says the gap is 30.50 against 23.64; the score says 28,557 against 3,366. Same 300 estimates, very different stories about how far apart they are.

Real world

Harrow Lane's review slide (constructed) said the new cleaning step, which drops rows with gaps, moved RMSE from 23.64 to 24.89: "within noise". The reliability engineer added two columns before signing: the summed score, 3,366 to 4,656, and the bias, +1.4 to +4.7 cycles. The step had not added noise; it had pushed the estimates late, on exactly the side the shop fears. The slide now carries four numbers and the cleaning step went back to practitioner session 6.

Self-studyWhy the gap is mechanical, not mysterious3 min▶

RMSE squares each miss, averages, and takes the root. Squaring is symmetric and grows like a parabola. The score exponentiates each miss, with a steeper constant on the late side, and sums. Both punish big misses more than small ones, so on the four main rungs they agree on the order. They disagree in three places: on the size of each step (an exponential stretches big misses far more than a square does), on direction (only the score sees it), and on close calls between rungs whose misses fall differently on the two sides, like rung 2 and the cap-130 check.

None of that makes one number right. RMSE is used here, not taught: rolling-origin backtests and error metrics live in learn time-series forecasting, backtesting and metrics. Scoring a detector's alarms with precision and recall is a different job again, in learn anomaly detection, measuring detection. The habit to carry from both: report the numbers side by side and say what each one cannot see.

Part 3 · build-along

Write the score, check it against the engine, then swap it

18 min hands on

Open the browser console on this page (the sample and the engine are already loaded). Six steps, each with the number you should see at seed 2008. You write the score in three lines, prove it agrees with the engine on all 300 units, then swap the two constants and watch the ranking move. The figure is the plan.

Write the score for one unit

Three lines, the figure's reading: early divides by 13, late by 10. Check the two anchors: a perfect estimate costs 0, and 20 cycles late (6.39) costs more than 20 cycles early (3.66). The same function in Python is in the second box, for your own notebooks; it prints the same three values.

consolefunction phm(d) { return d < 0 ? Math.exp(-d / 13) - 1 : Math.exp(d / 10) - 1; } [phm(0), phm(20).toFixed(2), phm(-20).toFixed(2)]
pythonimport math def phm(d, a_early=13, a_late=10): return math.exp(-d / a_early) - 1 if d < 0 else math.exp(d / a_late) - 1 [phm(0), round(phm(20), 2), round(phm(-20), 2)]

Check the lean against Figure 9

The plot reads about 147 at d = +50 and about 46 at d = -50. Yours should print 147.41 and 45.81, and the engine's own function the same 147.41. If yours printed them the other way round, you implemented the printed pairing.

console[phm(50).toFixed(2), phm(-50).toFixed(2), IOT.phmScore(50, 13, 10).toFixed(2)]

Score rung 3 yourself, on all 300 units

Run the bench once, then add your unit scores over rung 3's 300 predictions against the true remaining life at each cut. You should see 3366.18 twice and a gap of exactly 0: same arithmetic, same order. (Add in a different order and the gap may be a few parts in a trillion; that is floating point, not disagreement.)

Swap the two constants and score again

Write the literal reading of the printed equation and re-sum every rung. Rung 3 goes from 3366 to 5002; the planted dropout from 4656 to 4316, now the lowest on the bench; the baseline from 305167 to 6451096, because its two long-lived engines sit on the side that just got steep. Then ask the engine for the same swap: it prints 4315.57 for the dropout rung, which rounds to yours.

Pin the lean with a test that can fail

One assertion keeps your function on the side you chose; it prints nothing while it holds. Then ask the engine to plant the swap in its own scoring function and re-run its self-checks: exactly one fails, "late costs more than early at equal size". A check you have watched fail is a check you can trust.

consoleconsole.assert(phm(20) > phm(-20), "late must cost more than early"); IOT.selfTest("swapScore").checks.filter(function (c) { return !c.pass; }).map(function (c) { return c.name; })

The habit to keep. Before you quote any asymmetric score, probe it at d = +20 and d = -20, write down which side costs more and where the constants came from, and put RMSE, the bias and the late share beside it. A score with a direction is only as honest as your knowledge of the direction.

Check yourself

Three questions

A colleague's scoring function returns 3.66 for d = +20 and 6.39 for d = -20. What do you conclude?

Under the figure and the prose, +20 costs 6.39 and -20 costs 3.66. This function has them the other way round, which is the printed pairing read literally. It is a different score, not necessarily a wrong one (the same paper says the preference can be tuned), so the fix is to name the reading, not to throw the work away.

The mean-life baseline's summed score is 305,167 on 300 units under test. Which description is right?

The baseline leans early (43 percent late, bias -12.1). Its huge misses are false alarms on two engines that outlive the 200.37-cycle average. A sum of exponentials is ruled by its worst few units, so the per-unit average describes almost none of them.

With the two constants swapped, the planted dropout scores 4,316 and the clean rung 3 scores 5,002. What is the right reading?

Swapping the constants changes what is being rewarded, not how good the estimates are. RMSE has no side, so it does not move. The late shift the dropout causes is cheap under the swapped reading and expensive under the published one; which reading you report is a decision, and it decides the ranking.

Before session 8

Homework

Thirty minutes, half in the console on this page and half in your own code.

Probe a score you did not write

Find any asymmetric or weighted error metric in your team's code or in a public repository you rely on. Run it at d = +20 and d = -20, and write one line: which side costs more, which constant goes with which side, and whether the code, the docs and any plot agree. If they disagree, you have tonight's reading lesson in your own estate.

Weight the short remaining lives

The paper argues for weighting units with a short true remaining life more heavily. Add a weight to your total function (for example, double the cost of any unit whose true remaining life is under 30 cycles; the weight and the threshold are your choice, so write them down) and re-rank the eight rungs. Your numbers will differ from anyone else's; what matters is whether the order moves, and that the weight is a decision you can name.

Carry the deadline forward

Practitioner session 8 turns "late costs more" into a different shape: a decision that must arrive within a time budget, at the edge or in the cloud. Bring one sentence on how late a prediction can be, in your own setting, before it is worth nothing.
