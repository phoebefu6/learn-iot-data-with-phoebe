From warehouse to plant floor: the transfer map

Five sessions took a sensor dataset apart. This one turns the parts into a plan for a person. If you have shipped dashboards, pipelines or models on warehouse data, you already own skills a reliability team is short of, you are missing a handful of ideas that are routine there, and you carry a few habits that quietly hide the end of every engine's life. Tonight sorts them into three columns, draws each one at the station of a sensor-to-decision pipeline where it lands, from the device to the edge, the link, the model and the maintenance action, and ends with your own map and ninety days of free reading, with the official sources marked. Harrow Lane Overhaul, a constructed engine-overhaul shop, is hiring from data teams and needs the map too. The engine rows are real public rows from NASA's simulation, not real engines, and nothing here is purchasing advice.

Part 0

Harrow Lane, the week it hires from a data team

Harrow Lane Overhaul is a constructed shop: a planning lead, two reliability engineers and a hangar that can take a limited number of engines a week. Its fleet, as in every session, is the 100 turbofan engines of NASA's FD001 training set, simulated with C-MAPSS and run cycle by cycle until each one failed. The shop has budget for three hires, and the strongest candidates come from conventional data teams: an analyst from a retail warehouse, a data engineer from a payments platform team, and a data scientist who built churn and demand models. The planning lead's question is not whether they are good. It is which of their habits will help on day one, what they will have to learn, and which of their reflexes will make an engine look healthier than it is. That is the transfer map, and every cell of it sits somewhere a reading travels.

LiveThree columns, one pipeline2 min▶

A list of skills is easy to write and hard to use, because it does not say where a skill matters. So the map is drawn against the five stations every reading passes on its way to a decision: the device that takes it, the edge box that buffers and samples it, the link that carries it, the model that turns it into a remaining-life estimate, and the maintenance action a planner takes on that estimate.

  • Carries over. Skills a conventional analyst, engineer or scientist already has that are worth more here than where they were learned.
  • Must learn. Ideas that are routine on a reliability team and rare on a data team: sensor physics and units, sampling and clocks, missing not at random, censored run-to-failure labels and the capped target, asymmetric cost, edge or cloud budgets, a device security baseline, household privacy.
  • Must unlearn. Habits that are good practice on warehouse data and actively dangerous here, because on sensor data each one hides the end of a life or prices a mistake the wrong way round.

Leader session 5 named the five contrasts that break the warehouse playbook. This session is what a person does about them.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • Transfer map. Three columns, carries over, must learn, must unlearn, filled for one person and one role.
  • Station. One step a reading passes: device, edge, link, model, action. A skill is placed at the station where it does its work.
  • Edge. Compute that sits near the device, before the link. It can sample, summarise and decide without a round trip.
  • Censored label. A target you only know once the event happens. Before failure, every engine's remaining life is unknown, and in a truncated series it never becomes known.
  • Capped target. Remaining life clipped at a ceiling, 125 or 130 cycles in the literature on this data, so a model is only asked to track life near the end. A convention, not a law of engines.
  • Lean. Which side of the true failure a score punishes harder. The engine here follows the paper's figure and prose: late divides by 10, early by 13, so late costs more.
  • Official source. The publisher's or the author's own copy, free to read: NASA's repository, NIST's own site, the PHM Society's open proceedings, an arXiv author copy. Anything else is marked not official.
  • Constructed. Harrow Lane, its three hires and Flat 4B are invented so that the truth is knowable. The engine rows are NASA's simulation; no figure describes a real engine, plant or household.
Part 1 · covers what carries over and what must be learned

Skills that travel, and the ideas that are new

17 min live

The map first, then the left two columns. Read the figure a row at a time: each row is one station a reading passes, and the three cells beside it are what a data person brings to that station, what is new there, and what to drop there. The left column is fuller than most people expect. Profiling, time-series wrangling, reproducible pipelines, feature engineering, evaluation discipline and fleet dashboards all transfer. The middle column is where the physics, the clock, the censored label and the price of being late come in. The copper box at the bottom is the decision every column is judged at.

LiveCarries over: six skills worth more here5 min▶

Profiling and data-quality tests. The habit of looking at every column before modelling it is the habit a sensor feed needs most, pointed somewhere new. Instead of nulls and duplicate keys, the profile asks which channels are flat, which are noisy and which drift. In FD001, 7 of 21 channels never move under the one operating condition, and practitioner session 3 builds the triage table. Pipeline-level checks of freshness and volume are taught in learn data observability, builder session 5; here the drift is in the probe, not the pipeline.

Time-series SQL and dataframe work. Window functions, lags, as-of joins and resampling are the daily tools of practitioner session 2. Event time, late data and watermarks are taught properly in Windowing and Late data; a warehouse engineer who has written them is ahead.

Reproducible pipelines. Seeds, versioned inputs and a reference implementation that must agree to the digit. Every number in these sessions comes from a seeded engine that a second, independent implementation reproduces, and that discipline transfers whole.

Feature engineering and missing-data reasoning. Rolling windows, slopes and "was this value missing" as a feature are all used here; Missing data as signal teaches the general method. The question Rubin set out in 1976, when the process that causes missing data can safely be ignored, carries over as a question. What changes is the answer, which is the middle column.

Evaluation discipline. A holdout nobody touched, a baseline beside every model, time order kept, no later row informing an earlier prediction. Rolling-origin evaluation is taught in Backtesting and metrics. The bench in practitioner session 10 holds out 30 whole engines and scores 300 units under test, not the NASA test set.

Dashboards for a fleet. One row per engine, ranked by how soon it needs attention, with the uncertainty visible: that is a planner's screen, and a warehouse analyst has built its cousin many times.

Real world

Harrow Lane's first hire (constructed), the analyst from the retail warehouse, ran her usual profile on the fleet file in her first week. Every check passed. She did not stop there: she rewrote the profile as a triage, seven channels marked flat with the reason ("one operating condition, sea level"), fourteen marked moving, and one column the warehouse never needed, the cycle at which each engine's exhaust first reads in the hottest fifth. The skill was hers already. The questions were new.

LiveMust learn: the machine, the clock and the gap4 min▶

Sensor physics basics and units. Where a probe sits decides what it can see. The 21 channels in FD001 are read between the stages of a turbofan, in degrees Rankine, psia and rpm, as Table 2 of Saxena and colleagues (2008) lists them. A reader who cannot place a channel in the machine is modelling a column, not a measurement. Leader session 1 and practitioner session 1 draw the gas path.

Sampling and clocks. A sensor row has two clocks: the one the device samples on and the one the event happens on. In FD001 time is counted in operating cycles, not wall-clock seconds; in a live plant, channels sampled at different rates and stamped by drifting clocks must be resampled before they can share a row. Practitioner session 2 teaches it on constructed data.

Missing not at random. On warehouse data a null is usually a form left blank. On a device, a reading goes missing when the device struggles: heat, vibration, a full buffer, a weak link, which are the conditions nearest failure. Rubin's 1976 conditions for ignoring the missingness do not hold, and leader session 2 and practitioner session 6 show what deleting the gaps does to a remaining-life estimate.

LiveMust learn: the label, the score, the link, the device and the home4 min▶

Censored run-to-failure labels and the capped target. Each engine fails once, at the end, and until then its remaining life is unknown. A model trained on remaining life as is spends its effort on the healthy early cycles where nothing can be read; capping the target at a ceiling asks it to track life only near the end. On the bench, rung 1 to rung 2 is the move from an uncapped to a capped target, and it is the capped label and the clamp that do the work, not the combining of channels into one health index. The ceiling is 125 in some papers and 130 in others: a convention, chosen on this subset, never a law of engines. Practitioner session 5 builds both.

Asymmetric cost. A false alarm pulls an engine with life left in it; a missed failure leaves it in service. The PHM08 score prices the two differently: with the lean the paper's Figure 9 and prose describe, late divides by 10 and early by 13, so a late call of the same size costs more. Leader session 3 makes it a business choice, and practitioner session 7 takes the formula apart, including the printed equation that pairs the constants the other way.

Edge or cloud budgets. Every byte sent costs link capacity, and every round trip costs time between the sample and the decision. Practitioner session 8 is a constructed calculator for both.

A device security baseline. NIST IR 8259A (2020) names six device cybersecurity capabilities as a core baseline: Device Identification, Device Configuration, Data Protection, Logical Access to Interfaces, Software Update and Cybersecurity State Awareness. Its role, in its own words, is "as a default for minimally securable devices", and it "does not specify how" any capability is achieved. NIST SP 800-213 (2021) frames device requirements for federal organisations from the device's place in a larger system. Neither is a checklist to buy against; both are a vocabulary for asking a supplier what the device can do.

Household privacy. In a home the unit is a household, not a user, and a single electricity meter can print when people wake, leave, return and sleep. Leader session 4 and practitioner session 9 draw that line; the law lives in GDPR scope and PDPA consent, and nothing here is legal advice.

Self-studyThe 90-day ladder, source by source5 min▶

Free sources only. Official means the publisher's or the author's own copy: NASA's repository, NIST's own site, the PHM Society's open proceedings, an arXiv copy posted by the authors. Not official means anything else, to be checked against the published version. One rung a month, each ending in something you write.

Days 1 to 30, the machine and the rows.

  • Saxena, A., and Goebel, K. (2008). "Turbofan Engine Degradation Simulation Data Set." NASA Prognostics Data Repository, NASA Ames Research Center, Moffett Field, CA. Official: data set 6 of the NASA PCoE data set repository, which says "four different sets were simulated under different combinations of operational conditions and fault modes".   • Saxena, A., Goebel, K., Simon, D., and Eklund, N. (2008). "Damage Propagation Modeling for Aircraft Engine Run-to-Failure Simulation." PHM08, Denver, CO. Official: the PDF ships inside the same NASA zip. Read Table 2 (the channels) and section V (how the noise was added).

Part 2 · covers what must be unlearned

Seven habits that hide the end of a life

10 min live

Every habit in the right column was correct where it was learned. A null in a sales table usually is a blank form; incomplete rows usually are junk; a row usually is about one customer who agreed to the terms. Here each habit moves a number the planner acts on. Live"A null is missing at random" and "drop incomplete rows"3 min▶

A null is missing at random. On a device, a reading goes missing when the device struggles, and it struggles when the machine runs hot, which is at the end of its life. In FD001, 96.4 percent of readings in the last tenth of life sit in the hottest fifth, against 0.8 percent in the first tenth. A dropout driven by heat is therefore a dropout driven by the thing you are trying to predict. The replacement is a question, asked of the people who maintain the device: what does it do when it struggles, send nothing, repeat the last value, or send a default?

Drop incomplete rows. The planted fault in practitioner session 6 removes the hottest readings with probability 0.7, and 3,073 of 20,631 rows (14.9 percent) vanish. A pipeline that then drops rows with gaps and reads the last surviving row as current sees each engine cooler and healthier than it is. On the 300 units under test, from 30 held-out engines rather than the NASA test set, RMSE moves from 23.64 to 24.89 and the score from 3,366 to 4,656, with the bias moving late (+1.4 to +4.7 cycles). That score move assumes the lean the paper's figure describes, late divided by 10 and early by 13; read the printed equation literally and the score no longer ranks the dropout worse. RMSE gets worse under both. The replacement is to keep the gap as a fact: flag it, count it by engine and by condition, and never let the last row stand in for now.

Real world

Harrow Lane's third hire (constructed), the data scientist, reviewed a pipeline change in her second week. It dropped incomplete rows "for cleanliness", and the error moved by about 5 percent, which on a churn model she would have signed off. She asked for two more numbers before signing: the late-leaning score moved 38 percent and the bias moved late. She did not call the change wrong for every machine. She wrote that it made the shop's engines look healthier exactly when they were not, and asked which mistake the planning lead would rather explain.

Live"The row has an id" and "the user consented"3 min▶

The row has an id. A warehouse row is keyed to a customer or an order that exists on its own, and rows can be sorted any way you like. A sensor row is keyed to a machine and a moment, and its order is the life. Practitioner session 10's break button shuffles the cycle order inside each engine and runs the same model: RMSE 40.41 and score 46,145 on the 300 units under test, against 23.64 and 3,366 in order. Nothing in the table changed except the order. The replacement: the unit is the engine (or the household), every split is by unit, and every feature respects time.

The user consented. A meter account has one holder and one signature, and the trace it writes describes everyone who lives in the home. In the constructed Flat 4B trace, one threshold on one meter reads the household's state correctly in 93.0 percent of 15-minute slots and places the morning leave time within 30 minutes on 12 of 14 days: the schedule of an empty home. Giaconi, Gunduz and Poor (2018) list presence at home, religious beliefs, disabilities and illnesses among what meter data can reveal. The replacement is to decide where the data team stops before anyone asks it to go further; leader session 4 draws the line, and the law stays with the GDPR and PDPA sessions.

Live"RMSE is the score", "a trailing mean will do" and "more data to the cloud is free"2 min▶

RMSE is the score. A squared error is the same 20 cycles early or 20 cycles late. The planner's cost is not. The box above prints the PHM08 score for both sides: under the lean the paper's figure and prose describe, late costs more at every size. Report four numbers together, the error, the score with its lean named, the bias and the share predicted late, and crown none of them.

A trailing mean will do. The default smoother on a dashboard lags, and a one-way degradation signal punishes lag: on the bench a trailing mean of the last 10 values gives RMSE 24.88 and score 4,171, worse than not smoothing (24.00 and 3,542). A lag-free local line over the same window gives 23.64 and 3,366, a small gain of 0.36 in RMSE, not a fix. Ask which way a smoother leans before it ships.

More data to the cloud is free. Storage may be cheap; time is not. In practitioner session 8's constructed budget the cloud path takes 490 ms against a 500 ms deadline, 10 ms of slack that one slow round trip erases, while the edge path takes 140 ms. Arithmetic, not measurement, and your own link will differ; the habit to replace is sending everything because you can, rather than deciding which bytes need to leave the device.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree4 min▶

125 or 130? Zhou and colleagues (2020) cap the target at 130. Tsurkan and colleagues (2025) use 125, "based on the minimum run length of 128 cycles observed in the training data". A 2024 survey sets 125 citing Li, Ding and Sun (2018), and credits the piecewise-linear target to Heimes (2008), whose own cap value is reported, not read. On the bench, cap 130 gives RMSE 23.70 and score 3,622 against 23.64 and 3,366 for 125. A transfer map does not pick one; it carries both and says which was used.

Which way does the score lean? The printed equation in Saxena and colleagues (2008) pairs the divisor 10 with early errors and 13 with late ones, which, read literally, punishes early harder. Their Figure 9 shows about 46 at 50 cycles early and about 147 at 50 late, and their prose says late predictions "were more heavily penalized". The figure and the prose agree with each other; the printed pairing does not. Every later paper you read may have followed either, so check before comparing.

One score or several? The same paper argues for weighting short remaining lives more and for a correlation metric beside the sum, because a summed score can hide uneven performance across units. The bench prints four numbers and crowns none. Whether a single headline is ever enough is the disagreement to carry into your own reviews.

Part 3 · build-along

Your own transfer map, and ninety days of reading

15 min hands on

No code. You fill the three columns for yourself, in the variant that matches the job you hold now, with evidence from your own work in every cell. Then you turn the middle column into a ladder of free reading you can actually finish. The figure is the card to copy: three role variants on top, the 90-day ladder underneath.

Pick your variant and copy the blank map

Draw five rows, device, edge, link, model and action, and three columns, CARRIES OVER, MUST LEARN and MUST UNLEARN, as in the first figure. Then pick the role you hold now. An analyst usually brings time-series SQL and fleet reporting; an engineer brings pipelines, seeds and tests; a scientist brings features and time-ordered evaluation. The variant decides which cells you fill first, not which ones you may skip.

Fill CARRIES OVER with evidence, not adjectives

For each skill you claim, write the last time you used it and on what: "profiled every column of a returns table before modelling it", "kept a seed and a reference run for a demand forecast". A skill with no example goes in the middle column instead. Aim for four to six cells, each placed at the station where it lands.

Fill MUST LEARN in the order your role needs it

Eight ideas: sensor physics and units, sampling and clocks, missing not at random, censored labels and the capped target, asymmetric cost, edge or cloud budgets, the device security baseline, household privacy. Number them. An analyst usually starts with asymmetric cost, because a single accuracy figure is what an analyst is most often asked to report; an engineer with the link budget; a scientist with the capped target. Beside each, write the session that builds it.

Fill MUST UNLEARN with a habit you used last month

Of the seven, a null is missing at random, drop incomplete rows, the row has an id, RMSE is the score, a trailing mean will do, more data to the cloud is free and the user consented, pick the two you actually practise. Price the ones the box above can price, and write the number beside the habit. A habit with its price written next to it is much easier to drop.

Turn the middle column into a 90-day ladder

Copy the three rungs from the figure, keep the official markers, and put a date on each write line. Reorder the readings if your role needs it, but keep one written output per rung. For any source you find elsewhere, mark it not official and note what to check it against: for a remaining-life paper, its cap, its score's lean and whether it used the NASA test set.

Show the map to someone who will disagree

A reliability engineer, a maintenance planner, or whoever would review your first proposal. Ask them to move one item between columns. The item they move is the one your map was most wrong about, and it goes to the top of the first rung.

The habit to keep. Arrive with your old skills and your old questions, and leave your old defaults at the door. Every habit in the right column was a good habit somewhere else, and each one, on sensor data, either hides the end of a life or prices a mistake the wrong way round. Write the price beside the habit, name the lean beside every score, and read one rung a month from sources whose copy you can trust.

Check yourself

Three questions

Harrow Lane's engineer hire proposes sending every channel at full rate to the cloud, because storage is cheap. Which column of the transfer map does that habit belong in?

Pipeline skills carry over; "send everything" does not. Time between the sample and the decision is the budget the cloud path spends, and one slow round trip erases 10 ms. The numbers are constructed arithmetic, not a measurement of any link.

Which warehouse skill is worth more on a sensor feed than where it was learned?

Dropping rows with gaps removes the hottest, latest readings and leans the forecast late. One error figure cannot see which side of the failure a prediction lands. Profiling is the warehouse habit a sensor feed needs most, pointed at new questions.

A scientist drafts a 90-day reading plan. Which plan matches the ladder?

The ladder is free and says which copy is official. A lowest-error league table mixes caps of 125 and 130, scores leaning either way and different test protocols, so its numbers are not comparable. A summary is not the official copy, and on this data the printed equation and the figure of the same paper disagree.

After the track

Homework

Twenty minutes now, then one rung a month.

Finish the map and date the first rung

Complete every cell you can support with an example, put a date on the first rung's write line, and keep the map where you will see it. Re-read it at day 30 and move anything that changed column.

Price one habit on your own work

Take one result your team reported in the last quarter and ask which of the seven habits was in play. Write the four numbers it should have carried: the error, a score with its lean named, the bias and the share predicted late. If no habit was in play, write that down too; it is a finding.

Review one proposal at day 90

Use leader session 5's five contrasts, the source, the identity, the ground truth, the feedback loop and the cost of being wrong, on a real or constructed sensor-data proposal, with your transfer map beside it. The review is a judgement on a data claim, never a view on what to buy or which machine to service.
